\begin{abstract}
Lunar communications are no longer only a matter of individual space missions. With NASA's Moon Base program, announced in 2026, and China's International Lunar Research Station, the goal is a permanent link between Earth and the Moon for operating infrastructure on the surface. Such continuous service needs several layers: relays in lunar orbit, surface networks, and navigation and timing. NASA and the European Space Agency have contracted relay constellations, China operates a relay for the far side, and in 2025 Nokia attempted the first cellular network on the Moon, a 4G system on the Intuitive Machines IM-2 lander. NASA has since contracted a 5G system for the lunar surface. The contracted relays were specified for a few science missions and short crewed visits, and many choices for a permanent network remain open. This tutorial and roadmap addresses communications engineers who know cellular systems such as 5G or satellites in Earth orbit, but not necessarily space missions. We first compare what carries over from those systems and what changes, and review missions and traffic. We then describe the orbits and terrain that set link distances, show how the carrier frequency can be chosen on links that cross no atmosphere, and derive a scaling law between antenna size and distance for six links. Next, we cover the surface network, the relays, navigation and timing, protocols that tolerate interruptions, security, power and spectrum. Finally, we identify missing measurements and propose an architecture and a roadmap to 2035.
\end{abstract}

\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{F}{or} more than fifty years, communicating with the Moon has meant one spacecraft talking to one large antenna on Earth. The Apollo missions used direct S-band links to Earth, and every lander since then has done the same, except the Chinese landers on the far side, which reach Earth through a relay satellite. In March 2026, NASA announced that it will build a permanent Moon Base near the lunar south pole, in phases, with crewed landings from 2028~\cite{nasa_moonbase,nasa_art3_2026}. China and its partners plan the International Lunar Research Station~\cite{ilrs_relay_constellation}, and a 2024 tally counted 131 uncrewed cislunar missions announced or projected for 2024 to 2033~\cite{bukley2024moonstruck}. Many of these users will operate where Earth lies low on the horizon or is never visible. A rover behind a crater rim near the south pole may see neither Earth nor a relay, and the navigation satellites that give position on Earth are weak and poorly placed there. Serving such users requires a network: relays in lunar orbit, cells on the surface, a navigation and timing service, and protocols that hold data across the gaps in coverage.

NASA's Lunar Communications Relay and Navigation Systems (LCRNS) project aims at a commercial lunar communications and navigation service~\cite{lcrns_esper2025}, and in 2024 NASA awarded Intuitive Machines a contract for lunar relay services that include position, navigation and timing (PNT)~\cite{nasa_im_contract}. The Moonlight program of the European Space Agency (ESA) plans five satellites, one for communications and four for navigation, with initial operations targeted for the end of 2028~\cite{esa_moonlight_overview,esa_moonlight_csc}. China's Queqiao-2 relay has served the far side since 2024~\cite{cnsa_queqiao2}. On the surface, NASA has contracted a 5G system with integrated Wi-Fi~6 for the Moon Base~\cite{nasa_c26014}, and its Moon Base User's Guide lists surface-to-Earth links, links among surface assets and south-polar PNT among its functional gaps~\cite{nasa_moonbase_ug2026}. The spectrum is being decided at the same time: the World Radiocommunication Conference of 2027 (WRC-27) studies lunar allocations under its Agenda Item~1.15~\cite{wrc27_ai115}. So far, however, the only dedicated relays in operation at the Moon are Chinese, and of the nine landers that reached the surface since 2023, three landed upright and completed their surface operations (Section~\ref{sec:landers}).\footnote{Mission, contract, standards and schedule information is current to October 2026; later dates are agency or operator targets.}

The engineers who will build this network come from two practices. Satellite and deep-space practice schedules transmission and reception from predicted orbits and trajectories and accepts long propagation delays. Cellular and Wi-Fi practice relies on short distances, over which the radio adapts to the channel in a fast closed loop for synchronization, channel estimation and channel adaptation. A lunar network needs both, often on the same link chain. This tutorial explains the lunar network to communications engineers from either field, starting from what each already knows. It ends with a roadmap of the design decisions that fall in the next decade.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A function-by-function map from 3GPP NTN and deep-space practice to the lunar network, with the section that quantifies each difference (Sections~\ref{sec:bridge} and~\ref{sec:ntnbridge}).
\item The design inputs of a lunar network: the assets and the traffic demand, with every traffic class ordered by the delay it tolerates; the orbits and terrain that set link distances; and the carrier frequency from ultra-high frequency (UHF) to optical on links that cross no atmosphere, including the boundary between radio and optical links (Sections~\ref{sec:assets} to~\ref{sec:frequency}).
\item Budgets for six reference links, from surface access to direct-to-Earth, sized with the Friis equation written as a product of the two aperture diameters, with released code (Section~\ref{sec:budgets}).
\item The network functions that change on the Moon: surface coverage and handover, the relay constellation, positioning, navigation and timing without Earth GNSS, delay-tolerant protocols, security, power and spectrum. \subsection{Earth and Lunar Networks}
\label{sec:bridge}
A lunar network bridges the two practices introduced above. The relays, orbiters and landers follow deep-space practice: designers close each link on a predicted budget (received power computed in advance from geometry and hardware), and operators schedule contacts in advance. The surface network follows the practice of the 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP), the body that standardizes cellular systems: the radio measures its channel and closes a loop around the measurement. Where the round trip is too long for such a loop, as on the links to Earth, the lunar geometry is known in advance, so a prediction replaces the measurement. Table~\ref{tab:rosetta_radio} compares the two practices with the lunar network function by function, omits the functions that carry over unchanged, and gives for each row the section that quantifies the difference.

The relay links resemble geostationary (GEO) links in delay and Doppler. The contracted relays fly elliptical lunar frozen orbits (ELFOs), eccentric orbits whose high point, the apolune, dwells over the south pole. The near-rectilinear halo orbit (NRHO), a much larger orbit around the second Earth--Moon libration point, was selected for the Gateway station, now paused, and serves here as a reference (Section~\ref{sec:orbitfam}). Seen from the south pole, the delay to either relay stays within the range that the timing extensions of the 3GPP non-terrestrial network (NTN) specifications accept; only the links to Earth, at \SI{1.28}{\second} one way, exceed it (Section~\ref{sec:visibility}). The satellite engineer therefore knows the radio parameters of a relay link, and the 3GPP engineer knows how NTN handles its delay. New to both is the intermittent connectivity.

The Earth stands almost still in the lunar sky, but the Moon has no synchronous orbit, so no relay can stay fixed over a lunar site as a GEO satellite does over a terrestrial one. The Earth is never visible from the far side. There a relay is the only path, and the Shielded Zone, the far-side region protected for radio astronomy, constrains the emitters (Sections~\ref{sec:earthsky} and~\ref{sec:szgeom}).

The Earth end of every link is a small set of large antennas that many missions share (Section~\ref{sec:ground}). Each surface node operates only when its power source allows (Section~\ref{sec:power}). The lunar band plan falls under the same treaty of the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) as on Earth, but a consensus body of space agencies, the Space Frequency Coordination Group (SFCG), writes it, and contracts enforce it (Section~\ref{sec:spectrum}).

Readers from the two fields will find different sections new. The satellite engineer knows the method of Sections~\ref{sec:frequency} and~\ref{sec:budgets}, though not the lunar ranges or the freedom to choose the carrier. New to this reader are Section~\ref{sec:surface}, where a cellular network sits on terrain that divides coverage, and Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, where traffic passes from the 3GPP stack to a delay-tolerant stack. New to the 3GPP engineer are Sections~\ref{sec:geometry} to~\ref{sec:budgets}, where links close on a predicted budget instead of a measured channel, Sections~\ref{sec:space} and~\ref{sec:pnt}, where orbits and dilution of precision set the service, and the ground segment of Section~\ref{sec:ground}. Both readers need Sections~\ref{sec:cross} to~\ref{sec:roadmap} in full.

\subsection{What Carries Over from 3GPP NTN}
\label{sec:ntnbridge}
The 3GPP non-terrestrial network (NTN) specifications of Releases 17 to 19 extend 5G to satellites in GEO and LEO~\cite{lin2021ntn,giordani2021ntn,azari2022ntn}. A reader who knows them already has most of the vocabulary of a lunar network. What changes is the range of values and some of the assumptions behind them.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Feeder and service links.} NTN splits a satellite path into a service link to the user and a feeder link to a gateway on Earth. The lunar chain keeps this split: the service link runs from a surface user to a relay, and the trunk, from the relay to an Earth station, plays the role of the feeder link (Section~\ref{sec:sixlinks}).
\item \textbf{Transparent and regenerative payloads.} NTN distinguishes a satellite that only amplifies the signal from one that hosts the base station. A lunar relay must be regenerative, because it stores data between contacts (Section~\ref{sec:payload}).
\item \textbf{Delay and Doppler.} NTN accepts up to \SI{270.73}{ms} one way through a transparent GEO payload and pre-compensates LEO Doppler from the ephemeris. The relay links of a south-polar user fall inside both envelopes; only the links to Earth, at \SI{1.28}{\second} one way, exceed them (Section~\ref{sec:visibility}).
\item \textbf{Timing advance and GNSS.} NTN terminals compute timing advance and Doppler pre-compensation from their own fix of a global navigation satellite system (GNSS). That fix is weak on the near side of the Moon and absent on the far side, so a lunar service must supply position and time (Section~\ref{sec:pnt}).
\item \textbf{HARQ and store-and-forward.} NTN stretches the timing of hybrid automatic repeat request (HARQ) for GEO delay, and Release~19 adds store-and-forward for Internet-of-Things NTN. A lunar network needs store-and-forward for most traffic classes, across contact gaps of hours (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
\item \textbf{Direct-to-device access.} LEO constellations now serve unmodified phones from about \SI{550}{km}. The contracted lunar relays are about 30 times farther from a south-polar user, so handheld terminals need a surface gateway (Section~\ref{sec:sixlinks}).
\item \textbf{Inter-satellite links.} LEO constellations such as Starlink route traffic over optical inter-satellite links~\cite{chaudhry2021laser}. Lunar relays need the same function between satellites \SI{4100}{}--\SI{22400}{km} apart (Sections~\ref{sec:orbitfam} and~\ref{sec:frequency}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Related Surveys and Tutorials}
The literature on lunar communications is split across subdomains. Marine-Roig and Baeza~\cite{marine_roig_survey} present a systematic review of lunar communication and navigation systems and propose an integrated architectural roadmap. Serria \emph{et al.}~\cite{alqodah_review} review lunar surface communication and assess the antennas of lunar missions and research against propagation and radiation effects. Elewaily \emph{et al.}~\cite{islam_dtn_survey} survey delay-tolerant networking (DTN) for deep-space relay networks, and Liyanaarachchi \emph{et al.}~\cite{liyanaarachchi_cislunar} examine which 6G concepts carry over to cislunar networks. Kodheli \emph{et al.}~\cite{kodheli2021satcom}, Giordani and Zorzi~\cite{giordani2021ntn} and Azari \emph{et al.}~\cite{azari2022ntn} survey satellite communications and non-terrestrial networks from 5G toward 6G, and Lin \emph{et al.}~\cite{lin2021ntn} summarize the 3GPP NTN specifications. Jamshed \emph{et al.}~\cite{jamshed_ntn_tutorial} and Nguyen \emph{et al.}~\cite{nguyen_ntn_ojcoms2024} treat non-terrestrial networks in the wider satellite-aerial context. These works stop at Earth orbit; this paper starts from their concepts and follows them to the Moon (Section~\ref{sec:ntnbridge}). Caini and de~Cola~\cite{e2e_vs_dtn_2026} compare end-to-end Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) and QUIC transport with DTN over QUIC and Licklider Transmission Protocol (LTP) convergence layers on a common Earth--Moon testbed. Four recent reviews treat communication beyond Earth orbit more broadly. Ntontin \emph{et al.}~\cite{ntontin_procieee2025} set out a vision and roadmap for space communications in the 6G era that starts from the 5G non-terrestrial specifications. They cover satellite systems supported by artificial intelligence, space quantum networks, and joint communication and positioning, and they include LunaNet, NASA's interoperability framework for lunar networks, and Moonlight among the lunar use cases. Alshehhi \emph{et al.}~\cite{alshehhi_ojcoms2026} survey deep-space communication networks for missions beyond lunar orbit. Their coverage runs from the ground-station architecture through radio-frequency and optical physical layers and channel coding to the protocol stacks of DTN and of the Consultative Committee for Space Data Systems (CCSDS). Karmous \emph{et al.}~\cite{karmous_comst2025} survey deep-space optical communication, including hybrid radio-frequency/optical systems and interplanetary networking, and Ivanov \emph{et al.}~\cite{ivanov_ijscn2025} review the European deep-space optical programs, among them the Lunar Optical Communication Link. Ge\c{c}gel \c{C}etin \emph{et al.}~\cite{cetin_taes2026} propose lunar architectures in which near-Earth network assets serve surface users directly or through cislunar relays, and evaluate them with a link analysis that includes the lunar illumination cycle and an outage-based reliability metric.

The lunar surveys among these works catalog what was proposed and flown, the deep-space surveys address links whose range and delay exceed those of the lunar relay segment, and the non-terrestrial network tutorials address Earth-orbit and aerial platforms. Where these works compute lunar link budgets, as in~\cite{cetin_taes2026}, they give the outage probability and the required transmit power of selected architectures, without the required aperture as a function of orbit, frequency and data rate. Apertures and data rates are mission inputs there, not results of the link equation, so the relationship between orbit, aperture, pointing and power stays implicit. This paper makes that relationship explicit with a scaling law derived from first principles. The law supports trade-offs at system level, such as orbit against aperture, frequency against pointing and data rate against spacecraft power, that the mission-specific studies above do not address. The closest method is that of Saeed \emph{et al.}~\cite{saeed_ojcoms2021}, who state the radio-frequency and optical link-budget equations for satellite-to-satellite links in integrated satellite-aerial networks and plot the received power and $E_b/N_0$ against link distance. This paper extends that approach to the Moon. There the slant range spans five orders of magnitude and orbital mechanics constrains the aperture, a coupling absent from the satellite-aerial case. The terrestrial assumptions that the satellite-aerial case keeps (continuous connectivity, online authentication, handover triggered by fading) also fail on the Moon, each at a threshold that this paper quantifies.

\section{Flown and Contracted Lunar Assets}
\label{sec:assets}
Both radio traditions of Section~\ref{sec:bridge} are already in use at the Moon. Table~\ref{tab:assets} and Fig.~\ref{fig:assets_timeline} cover the lunar communication systems that have flown or are under contract. Every asset flown to date operates as a scheduled point-to-point link, with capacity allocated by a plan generated on Earth and routing fixed by that plan. \subsection{Orbital Relays}
The two relays that have supported far-side operations use different orbits. Queqiao occupies an Earth--Moon L2 halo orbit (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}), and its \SI{4.2}{m} deployable reflector returns at most \SI{555}{kbps} from the surface but forwards only \SI{125}{bps}~\cite{chen_ce4_relay}. Queqiao-2 retains the \SI{4.2}{m} aperture but exchanges the halo orbit for an elliptical frozen orbit with a period of \SI{24}{hours}, trading continuous far-side visibility for shorter slant ranges and hence higher rates~\cite{cnsa_queqiao2}.

Two orbiters not built as relays have nonetheless provided operational link data. The Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) carries a \SI{0.75}{m} combined S- and Ka-band aperture and returns \SI{100}{Mbps} at Ka, and its accumulated Ka signal-level record is, to the authors' knowledge, the most extensive published empirical lunar link record~\cite{lro_ka_analysis}. The Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter (KPLO) carried a delay-tolerant networking payload of under \SI{1}{kg} and \SI{5}{W}. The payload exercised the Bundle Protocol, the Licklider Transmission Protocol and file delivery between the Moon and Earth, including streamed video~\cite{kim_kplo_dtn}. It is the only in-flight demonstration reported to date of the protocol stack that Section~\ref{sec:protocol} analyzes. CAPSTONE, a 12U CubeSat, demonstrated crosslink navigation from its NRHO. It exchanged two-way coherent S-band ranging and Doppler signals with the standard transponder of LRO and processed them on board~\cite{capstone_caps,capstone_release}. The experiment demonstrated autonomous navigation of a small spacecraft at the Moon through a crosslink with an existing orbiter.

\subsection{Contracted Constellations}
The contracted systems all use elliptical frozen orbits, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:geometry}: with apolune over the southern hemisphere, the long dwell near apolune covers the south pole with a small number of satellites. The LCRNS reference design places five satellites in elliptical frozen orbits with periods near \SI{30}{hours} and specifies Ka-band user rates from \SI{1}{} to \SI{50}{Mbps}~\cite{lcrns_ref_const,nasa_lunar_relay_srd}. Its requirements document also sets a processing allowance for real-time data at each relay (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

Moonlight separates the two functions across the constellation, with one communications satellite and four navigation satellites (Section~\ref{sec:geometry})~\cite{esa_moonlight_overview,telespazio_moonlight}. With a single communications satellite at a fixed orbit, the band and the aperture that each user can carry set most of that user's rate. This dependence is the aperture-range scaling of Section~\ref{sec:budgets} evaluated at a fixed range. The JAXA navigation system uses elliptical frozen orbits, with eight satellites in two planes~\cite{jaxa_lnss}. China has announced the Queqiao communication, navigation and remote-sensing constellation, under feasibility study, to support the International Lunar Research Station and crewed lunar missions. Its published description gives a three-step deployment, with a pilot configuration around 2030, and candidate orbit classes; orbital elements and link parameters remain unpublished~\cite{ilrs_relay_constellation}.


\subsection{Landers and Direct-to-Earth Links}
\label{sec:landers}
Since 2023, nine landers reached the lunar surface. Chandrayaan-3~\cite{isro_ch3}, Chang'e-6~\cite{li2024ce6} and Blue Ghost Mission~1~\cite{firefly_bgm1} landed upright and completed their surface operations; SLIM~\cite{jaxa_slim}, IM-1~\cite{nasa_im1} and IM-2~\cite{nasa_im2} landed in an off-nominal attitude (tilted or on their side); and HAKUTO-R Mission~1~\cite{ispace_m1}, Luna-25~\cite{luna25_sfn} and Resilience~\cite{ispace_m2} were lost. Peregrine failed in cruise~\cite{nssdc_peregrine}, and Lunar Trailblazer lost contact the day after launch~\cite{jpl_trailblazer}. Artemis~II flew four astronauts around the Moon in April 2026~\cite{nasa_art2}, Chang'e-7 missed its planned 2026 launch window~\cite{xinhua2026ce7}, and the next commercial landers are targeted from November 2026~\cite{brinkmann2026}. Four of the landers above flew under NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program, which buys the delivery of NASA payloads from commercial providers: Peregrine and IM-1 in early 2024, and Blue Ghost Mission~1 and IM-2 in March 2025~\cite{nssdc_peregrine,nasa_im1,firefly_bgm1,nasa_im2}. As of September 2026, NASA expects the next commercial landers in this order: Astrobotic's Griffin-1, no earlier than November 2026; Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark~1 \emph{Endurance} and Intuitive Machines' IM-3 in the first part of 2027; and Firefly's Blue Ghost Mission~2 later in 2027~\cite{brinkmann2026}.
Except for the Chang'e far-side landers served by the Queqiao relays, every lander flown has communicated with Earth directly, at S-band (near \SI{2}{GHz}) or X-band (near \SI{8}{GHz}). The lander antenna is typically a steerable high-gain antenna of a few tenths of a meter, and the ground antenna is \SI{21}{}--\SI{70}{m}; IM-1 operated on its low-gain antennas after tipping over. The direct-to-Earth lander link is link~6 of Section~\ref{sec:sixlinks}. On that link the lander antenna tracks only libration, the small apparent motion of Earth in the lunar sky; terrain limits polar sites with Earth at low elevation, and the \SI{50}{MHz} X-band allocation limits the rate before the link budget becomes binding. Relays are required only where direct-to-Earth links are unavailable: the far side, the polar sites where Earth lies below a ridge, and users too small to host a steerable dish.

Table~\ref{tab:assets} lists the landers flown since 2023 with the link parameters their operators have published. Among these landers, Blue Ghost Mission~1 published the most complete link record, with an X-band antenna deployed after landing and more than \SI{119}{GB} returned~\cite{nssdca_bgm1,firefly_bgm1_14days2025}. IM-2 and the Chinese landers have not published their link parameters.

\subsection{The Earth Ground Segment}
\label{sec:ground}
The Earth end of the trunk and of every direct-to-Earth link is a small set of large antennas shared across missions. NASA's Deep Space Network (DSN) operates \SI{34}{m} beam-waveguide and \SI{70}{m} antennas at S-, X- and Ka-band (near \SI{26}{GHz}). Its three complexes are spaced in longitude so that one is always in view of the Moon~\cite{dsn_810_005}. NASA's Near Space Network supplements the DSN with government and commercial stations for lunar-distance support, and the European, Japanese, Indian and Chinese space agencies operate their own deep-space antennas. Station time is allocated by schedule across all missions in the network, so the trunk competes for the resource with planetary missions. Optical ground stations are fewer and are sited at locations of low cloud cover; the Lunar Laser Communication Demonstration (LLCD) used stations in New Mexico, California and Tenerife~\cite{boroson_llcd,giggenbach_locl}.

The Earth end is oversubscribed. Each DSN complex operates one \SI{70}{m} antenna and three or four \SI{34}{m} beam-waveguide antennas~\cite{dsn810005_101I,dsn810005_103F,dsn_34m_bwg}. Each antenna provides about \SI{540}{h} of schedulable time per 28-day interval. In 2023, requested time exceeded this capacity by up to 40~percent, and NASA's loading studies find that the Artemis missions double the capacity required~\cite{oig_ig23016}. Allocations vary by mission; published cases bound the range. LRO returns its science through about four \SI{45}{min} Ka-band passes per day at White Sands (between two and six as the lunar declination changes), plus one S-band pass per orbit~\cite{pds_lro_rss}. Artemis~I used more than 900 DSN hours in 25 days~\cite{oig_ig23016}. The Near Space Network adds commercial capacity: besides the relay contract with Intuitive Machines~\cite{nasa_im_contract}, NASA issued in December 2024 direct-to-Earth task orders to Intuitive Machines and Kongsberg Satellite Services in support of the lunar exploration ground segment~\cite{nasa_c24047}. The Lunar Exploration Ground Sites, three government-owned X- and Ka-band antennas at White Sands, in South Africa and in Australia, are intended to offload lunar traffic from the DSN~\cite{legs_brochure_r25,oig_ig23016}.

\subsection{Surface Networks}
The only attempt to date to operate a cellular network on the lunar surface is the Nokia system flown on the Intuitive Machines IM-2 lander. It integrated base station, radio and core functions into one enclosure and was intended to serve a rover and a hopper at ranges up to \SI{2}{km}. The system powered on, and telemetry confirmed that base station, radio and core were functional. The lander, however, came to rest on its side, and the resulting power and thermal state left the user terminals inoperable. No link to the terminals was established, and no user traffic crossed the cellular interface~\cite{nokia_im2_outcome}. The consequence for this paper is that no measured surface cellular link budget exists, and the numbers in Section~\ref{sec:cells} rest on the study that preceded the flight. Lunar Node-1 did return surface radio data, transmitting as an S-band ranging beacon to the DSN, although as a navigation payload~\cite{nasa_ln1}.


\subsection{Optical Terminals}
Optical links have demonstrated higher rates in lunar operation than the contracted RF systems specify. LLCD returned \SI{622}{Mbps} from lunar orbit through a \SI{0.107}{m} aperture radiating \SI{0.5}{W} average power at \SI{1550}{nm}~\cite{boroson_llcd}. That rate is an order of magnitude above the Ka-band services now under contract, and the aperture is an order of magnitude smaller than the relay reflectors of Table~\ref{tab:assets}. European ground stations received the same downlink at reduced rates set by the ground detector technology~\cite{giggenbach_locl}, and the Orion terminal delivered \SI{260}{Mbps} on Artemis~II in April 2026 from a comparable \SI{0.10}{m} aperture~\cite{nasa_o2o,nasa_o2o2026}.

This efficiency requires tight pointing, which the LLCD figures quantify: the terminal line of sight was stabilized to better than \SI{2.5}{\micro\radian} against a requirement of \SI{4.2}{\micro\radian} RMS, with a transmit beam of roughly \SI{15}{\micro\radian}~\cite{boroson_llcd}. Deep-space RF antennas operate with pointing errors measured in milliradians~\cite{dsn_810_005}, so the optical terminal demands roughly three orders of magnitude tighter control for the same link. The optical variant of Section~\ref{sec:link4} exhibits this pointing sensitivity. An optical budget that closes with a small aperture can therefore still fail on platform pointing. No optical link has been demonstrated between two spacecraft in lunar orbit, so the inter-satellite case has been studied only analytically.

\subsection{Interoperability Requirements}
The LunaNet specification, the SFCG recommendations and the CCSDS standards set the interoperability requirements. The LunaNet interoperability specification mandates version~7 of the Bundle Protocol together with its security extension, fixes the proximity and direct-to-Earth band plan, and defines real-time and store-and-forward service types without setting a numerical boundary between them~\cite{lnis_v5}. Frequency assignment follows SFCG Recommendation 32-2, which reserves \SI{2483.5}{}--\SI{2500}{MHz} for in-situ navigation with guard bands separating it from the adjacent terrestrial mobile allocation, and applies an occupied-bandwidth rule that directs links wider than \SI{6}{MHz} to Ka-band~\cite{sfcg_rec32}. The space link and file delivery layers follow CCSDS, which has adopted the delay-tolerant protocols as a companion series~\cite{ccsds_bp,ccsds_ltp,ccsds_lunar_standards}. A lunar radio design is thus constrained at the outset in band, bandwidth and protocol, and the parameters that remain free are those of Section~\ref{sec:frequency}.



\section{Lunar Users and Traffic Demand}
\label{sec:demand}
Lunar demand is unusual in two ways: it is small and sparse, and it was specified for the week-long crewed sortie, a mission profile that later plans extend to bases and commercial operations. This section quantifies the demand by phase and by traffic class and orders every class by the delay it tolerates, which constrains the architecture.

\subsection{Demand by Phase}
Table~\ref{tab:phases} summarizes four phases, with the published requirements where they exist and indicative values otherwise. There are about ten active orbiters, two to four landers a year, each operating for days to weeks, and no permanent surface user. Each is a scheduled point-to-point link; the individual rates are known (Table~\ref{tab:assets}) and no aggregate has been published. The infrastructure now under contract was specified for the crewed sortie. Its demand comprises the extravehicular activity (EVA) traffic of the study described below, an EVA Moon-to-Earth downlink requirement of \SI{20}{}--\SI{40}{Mbps}~\cite{nasa_arts_comm}, \SI{4}{GB/day} of science, rover teleoperation (Table~\ref{tab:traffic}) and relay user rates of \SI{1}{}--\SI{50}{Mbps}~\cite{nasa_lunar_relay_srd}. A base phase follows, with four crew for 30 to 60 days, a pressurized rover, a resource-extraction plant, robotic swarms and the first far-side instruments, and then a sustained international presence with commercial users and far-side observatories. The agency plans reviewed here foresee crews of a few people through the 2030s (Table~\ref{tab:phases}); the projected growth in demand comes from robots and instruments. Far-side radio astronomy arrays are expected to be the largest data producers on the Moon. Their raw rates, in the tens of gigabits per second, are reduced on site by orders of magnitude before return; the FARSIDE concept returns \SI{65}{GB} per day~\cite{burns_farside_2019}.

\subsection{Demand by Traffic Class}
A detailed public demand model for a lunar surface network is the Nokia Bell Labs study prepared for NASA~\cite{nokia_lunar_3gpp}. It considers up to two astronauts on extravehicular activity, with voice, telemetry and up to four simultaneous cameras, over a service area of approximately \SI{10}{km}. Each camera streams at \SI{3}{}--\SI{12}{Mbps} in 1080p or 4K High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC). In 3GPP terms the suit transmits on the uplink, toward the network, and the uplink requirement is near \SI{13}{Mbps} for one camera stream and \SI{26}{Mbps} for two. Against this demand the study evaluates 3GPP peak uplink rates from \SI{69.95}{Mbps} (Release~8, \SI{20}{MHz}) to \SI{166}{}--\SI{221}{Mbps} (Release~15 in time-division duplex, \SI{100}{MHz}). The spectrum assumed lies in the lunar surface bands recommended by the SFCG, including \SI{2.5035}{}--\SI{2.655}{GHz} and \SI{3.500}{}--\SI{3.800}{GHz}, with frequencies below \SI{2}{GHz} excluded to protect radio astronomy~\cite{sfcg_rec32}. The study concludes that both LTE and 5G~NR satisfy the mobile-broadband requirement for the planned surface traffic. The demand model remains an unvalidated prediction: the first surface network to fly did not complete a cellular call, and no in-situ traffic data have been published~\cite{nokia_lunar_3gpp}. Subsequent missions carrying updated surface networks and relay satellites are expected to provide the first operational data~\cite{im_ldn_constellation}.

The demand model above is sized for human extravehicular activity. Before crewed landings, all surface users are robotic, and the binding requirement of a teleoperated rover is control-loop latency. The Earth--Moon round trip of \SI{2.56}{\second} exceeds the reaction time of a closed driving loop, so a teleoperation link that crosses the trunk to Earth cannot close a fine control loop. An operator who sees an obstacle in the video feed cannot command a stop before the rover has covered the distance it travels in the \SI{2.56}{\second} round trip. NASA's Lunar Terrain Vehicle studies measured this effect: operators at delays of \SI{0}{} to \SI{8}{\second} all completed a \SI{6}{km} traverse, but average speed fell with latency and workload rose~\cite{ltv_teleops2025}. Either fine control closes locally, within a surface network that keeps the round trip below human reaction time, or the rover drives autonomously under supervisory commands that tolerate the trunk delay. In both cases the robot needs continuous coverage of its work area, but only at a low rate. The rates of Table~\ref{tab:traffic} suffice, and LiDAR-based safeguarded teleoperation has been demonstrated at \SI{1}{Mbps} with sustained rover speeds of \SI{0.15}{}--\SI{0.25}{m/s}~\cite{lidar_teleops2012}. The traffic is asymmetric, continuous during driving windows, and intolerant of interruption.

Autonomous multi-robot exploration adopts the second strategy, which relaxes the latency requirement on the Earth link. The CADRE mission is designed to demonstrate three rovers that receive only high-level commands from Earth and then explore, map and perform a multi-static ground-penetrating radar survey autonomously. The rovers communicate among themselves through a mesh network and with a base station on the lander~\cite{cadre_aero2024}. The Earth-link traffic is bursty: the rovers collect data during the lunar day and return it when the base station has relay visibility, which is the delay-tolerant model of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. The inter-rover mesh, by contrast, requires low-latency local exchange of state and map data at low rates and short ranges, which is a surface-to-surface link. Path-planning studies for such swarms show that the binding constraint is maintaining connectivity under terrain shadowing, with bandwidth secondary~\cite{banfi_multirover2024}. A rover that enters a shadowed crater therefore loses coverage in the same way as an astronaut.

Science data return from fixed or mobile instruments is the third class, and it is bulk transfer with no latency constraint, at \SI{4}{GB/day} for an initial science payload mission~\cite{nasa_arts_comm}. Science traffic can use any available contact window and suits the store-and-forward service. During crewed periods, however, it competes with teleoperation video for the same aperture, which is the contention the scheduling layer of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} must resolve.

\subsection{Classification of Traffic by Delay}
Fig.~\ref{fig:delay} plots the delay each regime of the chain produces, as vertical lines and bands, against the delay each class tolerates, as bars. A class can be served by every regime whose line lies to the left of the end of its bar. The rows of Table~\ref{tab:traffic} are ordered by tolerated delay, from swarm coordination to far-side observatory data, and three delay thresholds separate the classes. Below about \SI{100}{ms} one way, only the surface cell and a direct hop to an ELFO relay, \SI{29}{}--\SI{59}{ms} from the south pole, qualify. The relay hop qualifies only if its processing stays far below the \SI{1}{s} that the LCRNS requirements allow (Table~\ref{tab:chain}). Swarm coordination, fine teleoperation and telepresence are therefore local in practice. Between \SI{100}{ms} and about \SI{400}{ms}, the relay links qualify, so voice and interactive applications can run through an ELFO or NRHO relay but not through Earth. Above \SI{400}{ms}, only the links to Earth remain, at \SI{1.28}{\second} one way, and they qualify for the classes that tolerate it, namely bulk data and supervision, which are the traffic carried by store-and-forward. Earth--Moon conversational voice exceeds the \SI{400}{ms} limit of ITU-T G.114 by a factor of three and is therefore operated as push-to-talk regardless of link rate.

The safety-alert class of Table~\ref{tab:traffic} cannot wait for a scheduled contact, and the requirements address it through a separate low-rate layer and operating rules. The concept of operations for the crewed lander requires continuous communications in all crewed phases over dissimilar paths to Earth and tolerance of 12--24~h of unplanned loss~\cite{hls_conop008}. On the surface, suit alarms are broadcast locally to every crewmember, and loss of communication ends the extravehicular activity~\cite{eva_conops}. The relay requirements specify a critical-service availability of 98 to 99.5~percent (to be resolved, TBR) and a search-and-rescue service that detects a distress beacon within \SI{5}{min} and relays its message to Earth within another \SI{5}{min}~\cite{nasa_lunar_relay_srd}. LunaNet defines contingency signals at 15.625 and 250~symbols/s for nodes whose power or antenna pointing is degraded~\cite{lnis_v5}. Wherever an alert must cross the trunk, its delivery bound is therefore of the order of minutes, against the 1--3~s tolerance in Table~\ref{tab:traffic}, and the alarm path from suit to crew and lander must close locally.

\section{Geometry: Orbits, Terrain, the Earth in the Sky, and the Six Links}
\label{sec:geometry}
Before choosing a carrier or an antenna, a designer needs the range, delay and visibility of each link and the power cycle of each node, and the lunar environment fixes all of them. The relay orbits fix the ranges, delays and visibility. The terrain sets the surface horizon, and the position of the Earth in the lunar sky divides the Moon into near side, poles and far side. The lunar day sets the power cycle, and the Shielded Zone is a geometric definition with regulatory force. It then names the six links of Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} as pairs of platforms, classified by what each end can host and point, which is the input to the frequency analysis of Section~\ref{sec:frequency}.

\subsection{Orbit Families}
\label{sec:orbitfam}
The relay orbit is the first geometric design decision because it fixes the slant range, the distance from user to relay. The range enters the link budget quadratically through the free-space loss and the required aperture product linearly through the scaling law of Section~\ref{sec:budgets}. The Moon has no synchronous orbit, that is, no lunar equivalent of GEO. Its slow rotation, with a sidereal period of \SI{27.3}{d}, puts the synchronous radius at about \SI{88000}{km}. That radius lies outside the Hill sphere, the region of about \SI{60000}{km} within which the Moon's gravity dominates the Earth's, so no relay can remain stationary over a lunar site as a GEO satellite does over a terrestrial one (Fig.~\ref{fig:orbits}(b)). Five orbit families are flown or studied for lunar communications instead, each with a different trade-off between coverage, range and stationkeeping cost.

\emph{Elliptical Lunar Frozen Orbits} (ELFOs) in the family most often studied have perilune near \SI{900}{km} and apolune near \SI{8700}{km} altitude, inclined approximately \SI{56}{\degree} to the lunar equator~\cite{zhang_elfo_occlusion}; the LCRNS reference design uses a larger ELFO of approximately \SI{1750}{} by \SI{17400}{km} altitude with a period near \SI{30}{hours}~\cite{lcrns_ref_const}. The frozen geometry prevents secular drift of the argument of perilune under the Moon's oblateness ($J_2$) and the mass concentrations (mascons) below its maria, so the stationkeeping $\Delta v$ is small. Because apolune is placed over the south pole, a south-polar user sees the relay during its long dwell at ranges close to the apolune altitude, approximately \SI{8700}{km} for the smaller family and \SI{17400}{km} for the LCRNS design, while the short perilune passage occurs over the northern hemisphere; multiple satellites are needed for continuous coverage. The LCRNS reference design uses five ELFO satellites~\cite{lcrns_ref_const}, and Moonlight plans five satellites in elliptical frozen orbits: one for communications with a \SI{12}{hour} period and a semi-major axis near \SI{6000}{km}, and four for navigation with a \SI{24}{hour} period and a semi-major axis near \SI{10000}{km}~\cite{telespazio_moonlight}.

\emph{Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbits} (NRHOs) are a family of Earth--Moon L2 halo orbits with perilune altitude near \SI{1600}{km} and apolune altitude near \SI{70000}{km}. The long apolune dwell time over the lunar south pole gives near-continuous polar coverage from a single satellite and continuous coverage from two (Section~\ref{sec:space}), but its apolune range of approximately \SI{70000}{km} is four to eight times the apolune range of an ELFO, so the aperture product required for the same link is four to eight times larger. NRHO is the orbit selected for the Gateway~\cite{gateway_comm}, a program paused in March 2026~\cite{nasa_ignition2026}.

\emph{Earth--Moon L2 halo orbits} circle the second Earth--Moon libration point, which lies on the Earth--Moon line about \SI{64500}{km} from the lunar center, beyond the far side; Queqiao flies at \SI{47000}{}--\SI{79000}{km} from the Moon~\cite{chen_ce4_relay}, and because the orbit remains visible from Earth at all times, a relay on it sees both the far side and Earth continuously.

\emph{Low Lunar Orbits} (LLOs) are circular orbits at \SI{100}{}--\SI{200}{km} altitude. They give the shortest slant ranges but are strongly perturbed by mascons. Without stationkeeping, a \SI{100}{km} circular orbit lasts from about two weeks to more than two years depending on inclination~\cite{ramanan_llo}, and only a few frozen inclinations near \SI{27}{\degree}, \SI{50}{\degree}, \SI{76}{\degree} and \SI{86}{\degree} are long-lived~\cite{bell_lunar_orbits}. LLO therefore requires regular stationkeeping, as the operations of LRO show~\cite{lro_ka_analysis}.

\emph{Walker constellations} of frozen orbits can provide global navigation, with 16 to 24 satellites, three to five times the five of the regional designs. Navigation adds a second measure of coverage, the position dilution of precision (PDOP), the factor by which satellite geometry amplifies ranging error into position error; values below about 6 are usable (Section~\ref{sec:dop}). Iiyama and Gao~\cite{iiyama_lans} find that at least 99~percent global four-fold coverage requires 16 satellites in a four-plane Walker of circular frozen orbits, and that a PDOP of 6 or better over 99~percent of time and users requires 20 such satellites, or 24 in a Walker of north- and south-elliptical frozen orbits. The \SI{39.2}{\degree} inclination of the circular Walker, measured from the Earth--Moon orbital plane, degrades its high-latitude geometry, so that its polar PDOP is about twice its equatorial value~\cite{iiyama_lans}. Yuan \emph{et al.}~\cite{yuan_hybrid_cislunar} show that a hybrid of Earth--Moon L1/L2 halo, circular lunar and geostationary relay satellites outperforms Walker Star and Walker Delta constellations in age of information, the time elapsed since the latest delivered update was generated, at comparable coverage, and other studies combine L1 and L2 halo satellites for continuous south-polar service~\cite{mdpi_orbital_infra}.

Table~\ref{tab:orbits} summarizes the trade-off. Each orbit family suits a different link type: ELFO minimizes aperture for the surface-to-relay link, NRHO maximizes coverage per satellite, and a Walker constellation provides global navigation at the cost of many more satellites.

\subsection{Visibility, Dwell and Delay}
\label{sec:visibility}
What a user sees of a relay follows from Kepler's second law: a satellite moves slowest at apolune, its farthest point from the Moon. An eccentric orbit with apolune over the south pole therefore gives a south-polar user contacts of many hours at ranges near the apolune altitude, separated by gaps of a few hours while the satellite passes perilune over the northern hemisphere. For the LCRNS reference ELFO the user sees the relay for most of each \SI{30}{h} orbit; for the NRHO, apolune dwell lasts days at \SI{60000}{}--\SI{69400}{km}; for a low lunar orbit, passes last minutes at a few hundred kilometers. A relay path therefore requires one satellite in view, whereas a navigation fix requires four with adequate geometry.

Table~\ref{tab:satcom} places the service links to the two relay orbits beside the GEO and LEO references and the links to Earth. Its lunar values are seen from Malapert Peak (\SI{86}{\degree}\,S) above a \SI{5}{\degree} elevation mask without terrain, as 5th to 95th percentiles. The ELFO values come from the GMAT propagation of the LCRNS reference states~\cite{lcrns_ref_const} over \SI{90}{d}~\cite{asok2027space}, and the NRHO values from the 9:2 southern L2 halo, an NRHO that completes nine orbits every two lunar months, computed in the restricted three-body problem. The one-way delay to an ELFO relay near apolune is about \SI{60}{ms} and to an NRHO relay near apolune about \SI{230}{ms}, both below the \SI{270.73}{ms} that 3GPP accepts for a transparent GEO link~\cite{3gpp_tr_38821}. Because perilune lies over the northern hemisphere, a south-polar user sees the relay only on the slow part of its orbit. The range rate has a median of \SI{0.21}{km/s} for the ELFO and \SI{0.19}{km/s} for the NRHO, with 95th percentiles of \SI{0.44}{} and \SI{0.57}{km/s} and maxima of \SI{0.52}{} and \SI{0.65}{km/s}. The Doppler shift at \SI{26}{GHz} is therefore \SI{16}{} to \SI{49}{kHz} between the medians and the 95th percentiles, more than an order of magnitude below the $\pm\SI{620}{kHz}$ of a satellite in low Earth orbit (LEO) at \SI{600}{km}. Its rate of change stays near \SI{1}{Hz/s} against \SI{7}{kHz/s} for LEO. Only the links to Earth, at \SI{1.28}{\second} one way, exceed the GEO range.

\subsection{Terrain and the Surface Horizon}
The Moon has neither atmosphere nor ionosphere, so the radio horizon coincides with the geometric horizon at $d = \sqrt{2 h R_{\mathrm{M}}}$ for mast height $h$ and lunar radius $R_{\mathrm{M}} = \SI{1737}{km}$. A \SI{10}{m} mast therefore reaches \SI{5.9}{km} and a \SI{100}{m} mast reaches \SI{18.6}{km}, in both cases before terrain is taken into account. Node density in a lunar surface network is consequently set by terrain, whereas in dense terrestrial deployments it is set by co-channel interference.


Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon} plots this horizon against antenna height. The curve motivates the mountain-top repeaters of Section~\ref{sec:architecture}: a \SI{2}{km} massif reaches \SI{83}{km}, fourteen times the horizon of a \SI{10}{m} mast, and the polar massifs are also the sites with near-permanent sunlight (Section~\ref{sec:power}).

Two further surface properties have second-order effects relative to blockage. The dielectric permittivity of the regolith follows $\varepsilon_r' = (1.93 \pm 0.17)^{\rho}$ for bulk density $\rho$ in \si{g/cm^3}~\cite{olhoeft_strangway1975}, with a loss tangent between $10^{-3}$ in the highlands and $10^{-2}$ in the mare basalts at S-band; measurements on Chang'e-5 samples extend these figures to \SI{0.2}{}--\SI{12.2}{GHz}~\cite{dong_dielectric_ce5}. A surface antenna therefore operates above a ground plane with $\varepsilon_r$ between 2 and 3, which perturbs the radiation pattern. Median surface slopes range from \SI{3}{}--\SI{5}{\degree} in the maria to \SI{5}{}--\SI{10}{\degree} in the highlands on a \SI{1}{km} baseline~\cite{rosenberg_terrain2011}.


\subsection{The Earth in the Lunar Sky: Near Side, Poles, Far Side}
\label{sec:earthsky}
The Moon rotates once per orbit, so the Earth stands nearly still in the lunar sky. It moves only through libration, the slow apparent rocking of the Moon by about \SI{\pm 7}{\degree} over a month that follows from its elliptical and inclined orbit. This near-fixed position divides the Moon into three design cases. On the near side Earth is always above the horizon, at an elevation set by the site's longitude and latitude. A large, fixed or slowly tracking antenna reaches it, as a GEO ground terminal reaches its satellite, and every near-side lander has therefore used a direct-to-Earth link (Table~\ref{tab:assets}). At the poles Earth lies within a few degrees of the horizon and rises and sets with libration, so a crater rim, a ridge or the lander's own structure blocks it for part of each month and a relay is needed for continuity. On the far side Earth remains below the horizon, so all traffic transits a relay at any aperture or power. No signal from an Earth global navigation satellite system (GNSS) arrives, so position and time must come from a lunar service or from ranging with the relay. The Chang'e-4 and Chang'e-6 missions are the only ones to have operated there, both through Queqiao relays in orbits chosen so that the relay sees Earth and the far side simultaneously.

\subsection{The Lunar Day}
The lunar day is \SI{29.5}{Earth-days} with a night of \SI{14.77}{Earth-days}~\cite{lunar_sourcebook}, so a solar-powered node must store or forgo energy for \SI{354}{hours} of darkness. Near the poles the sun circles the horizon at a few degrees of elevation, so the best ridge sites are lit 92 to 96 percent of the time at a few meters above the ground, with darkness lasting up to a few days (Section~\ref{sec:power}). The floors of permanently shadowed craters receive no direct sunlight. Surface temperatures range from \SI{390}{K} at lunar noon to \SI{95}{K} before sunrise at the equator~\cite{williams_thermal2017}, the coldest permanently shadowed regions reach approximately \SI{30}{}--\SI{40}{K}~\cite{paige_psr2010}, the surface absorbed dose rate is \SI{13.2}{\micro\gray\per\hour}~\cite{zhang_radiation2020}, and the annual galactic cosmic-ray dose equivalent is \SI{416}{mSv}~\cite{naito_radiation}. The lunar day therefore determines which nodes are powered (Section~\ref{sec:power}), what thermal range the electronics must survive, and which sites can host infrastructure.

\subsection{The Shielded Zone as Geometry}
\label{sec:szgeom}
The Shielded Zone of the Moon comprises the area of the lunar surface lying more than \SI{23.2}{\degree} beyond the mean limb as seen from the center of the Earth, together with an adjacent volume of space. Both are shielded from emissions originating within \SI{100000}{km} of the center of the Earth~\cite{itu_rr2024,itu_ra479} (Fig.~\ref{fig:szm}). The far-side band within \SI{23.2}{\degree} of the mean limb therefore lies outside the zone. It is a geometric definition with regulatory force: within it, No.~22.22 of the Radio Regulations protects radio astronomy and the other passive services across the whole spectrum, with the exemptions set out in Section~\ref{sec:spectrum}. Because libration moves the limb by \SI{\pm 7}{\degree}, the zone is defined from the mean limb and its edge is a band of the surface that is sometimes Earth-visible and sometimes not.

\subsection{The Six Links as Pairs of Platforms}
\label{sec:links}
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} names six links, and Table~\ref{tab:links} gives their ranges. The input to the frequency analysis includes, besides the range, the nature of the two ends, because the carrier a link can use depends on what each end can host and point, and four platform classes cover the six links. An \emph{omnidirectional} end, a rover, a suited astronaut or an emplaced instrument, hosts an antenna of a few decibels of gain and cannot point. A \emph{pointable, mass-limited} end, a relay dish or a lander antenna, hosts a reflector of a fraction of a meter to a meter and points it to a few tenths of a degree with a gimbal. A \emph{fixed or tracked} end, a surface gateway or a relay serving another relay, hosts a larger aperture and tracks a slowly moving target to a hundredth of a degree. A \emph{microradian} end is an optical terminal with a fine-steering mirror and a beacon loop. In these terms, link~1 (surface access) is omnidirectional at both ends. Link~2 (surface to relay) joins a pointable lander or rover dish to a pointable relay; an omnidirectional user cannot close it (Section~\ref{sec:sixlinks}). Link~3 (gateway backhaul) and link~4 (inter-satellite) are pointable or tracked at both ends. Link~5 (trunk) is pointable at the relay and tracked by a very large ground antenna; link~6 (direct-to-Earth) is pointable at the lander and tracked on Earth. Section~\ref{sec:frequency} assigns a frequency to each link from these classes.


\section{The Frequency Axis: Propagation, Gain, Bandwidth, Hardware and Regulation}
\label{sec:frequency}
With the geometry fixed, the carrier frequency is the remaining design variable. The links among the lunar surface and the relays cross no atmosphere. On Earth, atmospheric absorption and rain shaped the band windows, so the terrestrial band plan has no propagation basis on these links. The carrier is set instead by propagation, antenna gain, bandwidth, hardware and regulation. Propagation is free space everywhere except the Earth end of a trunk. Antenna gain and beamwidth scale with $D/\lambda$, and the allocation sets the bandwidth. In hardware, transmit power and noise temperature degrade with frequency at rates set by technology. Regulation adds the allocations, the Shielded Zone and the passive bands. This section puts all five on one axis, from UHF to optical, and determines the position of each of the six links on that axis. The aperture-range law gives the product of the two dish diameters needed for a given range, wavelength, noise, rate and power. Its proportional form suffices here, $D_t D_r \propto R\lambda\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{sys}} R_b / P_t}$; Section~\ref{sec:budgets} derives the exact form. Geometry fixes $R$; this section is about $\lambda$ and about how $P_t$ and $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ move with it. Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the symbols used here and in Section~\ref{sec:budgets}.

\begin{table}[!t]
\caption{Symbols Used in the Link Equations}

\subsection{Propagation: Free Space, Except at the Earth End}

A transmitter radiating power $P_t G_t$ uniformly over a sphere of radius $R$ produces a power flux density $P_t G_t / (4\pi R^2)$ at the receiver. A receiving antenna of effective area $A_e = G_r \lambda^2 / (4\pi)$ therefore captures $P_r = P_t G_t G_r \lambda^2 / (4\pi R)^2$, from which the free-space path loss follows as
\begin{gather}
\eqname{Free-Space Path Loss}\\
L_{\mathrm{FS}} = \left(\frac{4\pi R}{\lambda}\right)^{2} = \left(\frac{4\pi R f}{c}\right)^{2}.
\label{eq:fspl}
\end{gather}
In decibels the loss increases by \SI{20}{dB} per decade of range and by \SI{20}{dB} per decade of frequency.


On the Moon, free-space loss is the complete propagation model for any unobstructed path. For radio purposes the exosphere is a vacuum, with no rain, gaseous absorption, ionosphere or tropospheric scintillation. A link between a surface gateway and a relay, or between two relays, therefore suffers only free-space loss at any frequency from UHF to the optical bands. Terrain is the only obstruction (Section~\ref{sec:surface}), and the regolith ground plane perturbs a surface antenna's pattern at second order. The atmosphere re-enters only at the Earth end of the trunk and of the direct-to-Earth link. There, rain attenuates Ka-band, and water vapor absorbs the bands above \SI{100}{GHz} except in a few windows at dry high-altitude sites. Cloud blocks optical links (Section~\ref{sec:budgets}). The terrestrial band structure therefore applies to only two of the six lunar links.

\subsection{Antenna Gain and Beamwidth}
\label{sec:gain}

For a circular aperture of diameter $D$ and efficiency $\eta$, the gain and the half-power beamwidth, the latter for the tapered illumination typical of reflector antennas (uniform illumination gives $58.4\lambda/D$), are
\begin{gather}
\eqname{Aperture Gain and Beamwidth}\\
G = \eta \left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^{2}, \qquad \theta_{3\mathrm{dB}} \approx \frac{70\lambda}{D} \ \ [\mathrm{deg}].
\label{eq:gain}
\end{gather}
Both relations hold for apertures large compared with the wavelength, $D \gg \lambda$; below that the gain formula fails and the antenna is a low-gain radiator. By \eqref{eq:gain}, doubling the aperture diameter raises the gain by \SI{6}{dB} and halves the beamwidth. The aperture that delivers the gain therefore also sets the pointing requirement. Doubling the carrier frequency raises the gain by \SI{6}{dB} at each fixed aperture and raises the path loss by \SI{6}{dB}. The net effect on margin depends on which quantities are held fixed. If both antennas are fixed in physical aperture, the two gain increments (\SI{12}{dB}) exceed the path-loss increment (\SI{6}{dB}) and the margin improves by \SI{6}{dB} per octave. If both antennas are fixed in gain, the path loss dominates and the margin degrades by \SI{6}{dB} per octave. If one antenna is fixed in aperture and the other in gain, the two effects cancel and the margin is unchanged. The first case applies to a lunar trunk link, where both the spacecraft dish and the ground antenna are specified by diameter. A higher frequency then increases the margin, which explains the preference for Ka-band over S-band.

\begin{remark}[Frequency Selection as an Aperture Decision]
\label{rem:freq}
The carrier should follow from what limits each end of a link. Where both antennas are limited in size, a higher frequency increases the margin; where one end is a wide-beam terminal limited in gain, it reduces the margin. We use the term \emph{aperture rule} for this design rule. The selection itself is made on the ladder of Section~\ref{sec:ladder}.
\end{remark}


\subsection{Bandwidth: What the Allocation Grants}
The achievable rate is the smaller of the power-limited and the bandwidth-limited rates, and the second is set by regulation.


S-band (\SI{2.0}{}--\SI{2.7}{GHz}) is the usual band for surface access and navigation. Its \SI{11}{}--\SI{15}{cm} wavelength permits omnidirectional or low-gain antennas on rovers and suited astronauts. The SFCG recommends the \SI{2.400}{}--\SI{2.480}{GHz} and \SI{2.5035}{}--\SI{2.655}{GHz} bands for lunar surface wireless networks and the \SI{2483.5}{}--\SI{2500}{MHz} band for in-situ lunar navigation~\cite{sfcg_rec32}. The same recommendation also lists UHF bands (restricted to use outside the Shielded Zone), \SI{3.5}{}--\SI{3.8}{GHz}, \SI{5.15}{}--\SI{5.835}{GHz}, \SI{5.855}{}--\SI{5.925}{GHz}, \SI{25.25}{}--\SI{25.5}{GHz} and \SI{27.5}{}--\SI{28.35}{GHz} for surface networks. S-band is therefore the preferred surface option, though not the only one. S-band offers limited bandwidth: within the SFCG-recommended bands the 3GPP peak uplink rates run from about \SI{70}{} to about \SI{220}{Mbps} (Section~\ref{sec:demand}).

K/Ka-band is the usual band for the trunk and backhaul links. The trunk uses \SI{22.55}{}--\SI{23.15}{GHz} Earth-to-space and \SI{25.5}{}--\SI{27.0}{GHz} space-to-Earth; the proximity links and crosslinks use \SI{23.15}{}--\SI{23.55}{GHz} forward and \SI{27.0}{}--\SI{27.5}{GHz} return~\cite{sfcg_rec32,lnis_v5}. The reason is gain: at the \SI{11.5}{mm} wavelength a \SI{1.5}{m} dish produces \SI{50}{dBi}, which the \SI{384400}{km} Earth--Moon path needs. In exchange, the \SI{0.54}{\degree} beamwidth of a \SI{1.5}{m} Ka antenna requires attitude knowledge and control to \SI{0.1}{\degree}. That requirement is within the capability of a relay satellite and beyond that of a rover. Ka-band also suffers atmospheric attenuation at the Earth ground station. At \SI{26}{GHz} and 99.9~percent availability, this attenuation reaches approximately \SI{9}{}--\SI{19}{dB} at \SI{20}{\degree} elevation at the DSN complexes (Section~\ref{sec:trunk})~\cite{itu_p618,islam_ka_band}.

X-band (\SI{7.19}{}--\SI{8.5}{GHz}) is a compromise between S and Ka, offering \SI{4}{cm} wavelength with moderate pointing requirements. Landers that lack a steerable Ka dish use X-band for direct-to-Earth links. X-band also carries the relay links between Queqiao, the Chang'e-4 relay satellite, and the far-side lander and rover. Queqiao returns data to Earth at S-band to avoid co-channel interference with the X-band relay return in \SI{8450}{}--\SI{8500}{MHz}~\cite{chen_ce4_relay}. Resolution~680 of the World Radiocommunication Conference of 2023 (WRC-23) lists frequency ranges in which to study the spectrum needs of space research service systems on the lunar surface and between lunar orbit and the surface. The list includes \SI{7190}{}--\SI{7235}{MHz} and \SI{8450}{}--\SI{8500}{MHz}~\cite{itu_wrc23_res680}.

Optical (\SI{1064}{}--\SI{1550}{nm}) is selected for the inter-satellite link and the high-rate trunk when pointing stability permits. The \SI{1.55}{\micro\m} wavelength provides approximately \SI{105}{dBi} gain from a \SI{10}{cm} aperture with an efficiency of 0.8. The beam is correspondingly narrow: its diffraction-limited divergence $1.22\lambda/D \approx \SI{19}{\micro\radian}$ is approximately 500 times narrower than the \SI{0.54}{\degree} Ka-band beam, and the required pointing stability is finer by the same factor. Optical links are unaffected by RF spectrum congestion. They also lie outside the Shielded Zone provisions of Article~22, because the Radio Regulations apply to radio waves of frequencies below \SI{3000}{GHz} (No.~1.5)~\cite{itu_rr2024}. Clouds block them, however, so they are restricted to the space-to-space segment or to dry-site ground stations.


No lunar-specific allocation or recommendation exists above the Ka-band ranges: the SFCG recommendation and the WRC-27 study ranges of Resolution~680 stop at \SI{28.35}{GHz} (Section~\ref{sec:spectrum}). The Radio Regulations do allocate inter-satellite and space research bands above \SI{30}{GHz}, at Q/V-band, E-band, W-band and in several windows of the D-band and sub-terahertz ranges. These allocations were written for Earth orbit and have not been examined for lunar use. Above \SI{100}{GHz}, the passive bands reserved for radio astronomy and Earth exploration are dense. Inside the Shielded Zone, No.~22.22 protects them, with the exemptions of Section~\ref{sec:spectrum}. The bandwidth each band offers a lunar link is therefore a regulatory variable with three states: allocated and recommended for the Moon (S, X, Ka), allocated for space use but without lunar provisions (Q/V to sub-terahertz), and unallocated (optical, which lies above the \SI{3000}{GHz} limit of the Regulations).

\subsection{Hardware: Power and Noise Versus Frequency}
Hardware also changes with frequency. Space-qualified transmit power falls with frequency, and receiver noise rises with it. Because the achievable rate scales as $P_t G_t G_r / T_{\mathrm{sys}}$, these two quantities set the practical top of the frequency ladder. Table~\ref{tab:ladder} collects the state of practice by band, with representative published hardware values and their status; its Earth-end column is the clear-sky zenith gaseous attenuation of ITU-R P.676~\cite{itur_p676_13} at Goldstone and at a \SI{5}{km} site for median water vapor. Up to Ka-band, traveling-wave tube amplifiers (TWTAs) and gallium-nitride solid-state power amplifiers (SSPAs) deliver tens to hundreds of watts in flight at 40 to 60~percent DC-to-RF efficiency. Satellite tubes exceed 70~percent tube efficiency~\cite{menninger2005,lohmeyer2016}. Above Q/V-band, tubes have only laboratory heritage. Laboratory tubes deliver tens of watts up to about \SI{263}{GHz}, mostly pulsed. Solid-state amplifiers fall from watts at E-band to about 0.1~W at D-band and 10~mW near \SI{300}{GHz}~\cite{paoloni2026,maiwald2023}. The receiver noise temperatures assumed here run from about \SI{500}{K} at Ka-band to \SI{1500}{K} at D-band and several thousand kelvin at \SI{300}{GHz}. Optical terminals sit outside this progression, with fiber amplifiers of watts and receivers that need tens of photons per bit~\cite{caplan_laser_comm}.

The second hardware variable is the power the platform can supply. A relay smallsat of a few hundred kilograms typically has payload power of a few hundred watts to a kilowatt. That bus power, multiplied by the amplifier's DC-to-RF efficiency, caps $P_t$. With the efficiencies above, \SI{100}{W} of DC power is \SI{40}{}--\SI{50}{W} of RF at Ka-band, about \SI{20}{W} at W-band, an estimated \SI{10}{}--\SI{15}{W} at D-band and about \SI{2}{W} at \SI{290}{GHz}. \subsection{Boundary Between Radio and Optics}
On Earth the bands above \SI{100}{GHz} are a compromise: more bandwidth than below \SI{100}{GHz}, more reliability through weather than optics, and pointing requirements two orders of magnitude looser than a laser terminal. On the Moon the weather argument applies only to the trunk, and the comparison between radio and optics reduces to the following factors. Pointing: a \SI{1}{m} dish at \SI{300}{GHz} has a beamwidth of about \SI{1.2}{mrad}, about twelve times narrower than at Ka-band but sixty times wider than the \SI{19}{\micro\radian} beam of a \SI{10}{cm} optical terminal. Acquisition and reacquisition after an occultation therefore use the attitude knowledge of the platform rather than a beacon loop. Dust: regolith grains of tens of micrometers are opaque to \SI{1.55}{\micro\metre} light and transparent Rayleigh scatterers to a \SI{1}{mm} wave, and lunar dust is electrostatically levitated, adhesive and abrasive, so a radio aperture is expected to tolerate a dust coating that would degrade an optical terminal. Thermal tolerance of the terminal and continuity with the Ka-band radio architecture complete the list.

On the Moon, sub-terahertz is therefore the upper end of the radio ladder, and the design question is the frequency above which optics is preferred. On the trunk the atmosphere at the Earth end applies again, and so does the terrestrial trade-off: Ka-band offers the higher availability and optical the higher rate, with the bands between them usable only from dry high sites that the deep-space networks do not occupy. Two boundaries are therefore distinct: the upper limit of the radio ladder is set by source power and lies near \SI{240}{GHz} for links 3 and 4 with the laboratory tubes of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, and the choice between that limit and optics is set by pointing, with dust as a further constraint on optical terminals.

Asok \emph{et al.}~\cite{asok2027space} quantify the second boundary for \SI{290}{GHz} against \SI{1550}{nm} over the propagated LCRNS crosslinks, a radio case just above the range in which the ladder rate stays within \SI{1.5}{dB} of its maximum, and therefore conservative for radio. Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}(b) recomputes that result as the largest per-axis pointing jitter at which each band holds a given rate with 99.9~percent availability and a \SI{3}{dB} margin, with \SI{0.3}{m} apertures and \SI{1}{W} at the mean (\SI{16809}{km}) and worst (\SI{22433}{km}) crosslink ranges; the circle marks where the two bands deliver the same rate. Optical holds the most margin, but it keeps 99.9~percent availability only below about \SI{3}{\micro\radian} of per-axis pointing jitter, and at its \SI{50}{Gbps} modem cap only below \SI{1.5}{\micro\radian}. The \SI{290}{GHz} link with these terminals is limited to about \SI{63}{Mbps} at the mean range, but it holds that rate within \SI{0.15}{dB} up to about \SI{100}{\micro\radian} of jitter, beyond a star-tracker-class pointing budget. The fixed pointing error at which the two rate ceilings cross is \SI{9.8}{\micro\radian}, independent of range, and the two availability contours cross at \SI{2.6}{\micro\radian} of jitter.

\subsection{Reuse Versus Optimality}
The ladder analysis is compatible with reusing existing radios on many links. Table~\ref{tab:ladder} includes heritage because S-, X- and Ka-band transponders have deep-space flight history and ground segments that already receive them; Ku-, Ka- and E-band radios and optical inter-satellite terminals are mass-produced for Earth-orbit megaconstellations and can be reused; D-band radios exist for terrestrial backhaul and are being qualified for space. The conclusion is narrower: for the links that lie entirely in vacuum, the band should be chosen on the ladder for what the two ends can point and the platform can power, and the heritage column indicates the present cost of that choice.


\section{Link Budgets: One Equation, Six Links}
\label{sec:budgets}
A link budget adds up every gain and loss between transmitter and receiver to check whether a link delivers enough signal. This section builds the budgets from the geometry of Section~\ref{sec:geometry} and the frequency axis of Section~\ref{sec:frequency}. It states the link equation and derives from it the aperture-range scaling law that organizes the paper. It then evaluates one budget in full, generalizes it through parametric curves, sizes the six links, and ends with the decision-flow procedure a designer follows.

\subsection{The Link Equation}

A link works when each received bit carries enough energy relative to the noise. The ratio of received energy per bit to noise spectral density is
\begin{gather}
\eqname{Link Budget Equation}\\
\frac{E_b}{N_0} = \frac{P_t \, G_t \, G_r}{k \, T_{\mathrm{sys}} \, R_b \, L_{\mathrm{o}}} \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^{2},
\label{eq:ebn0}
\end{gather}
in which $P_t$ is the transmit power in watts, $G_t$ and $G_r$ are the transmit and receive antenna gains, $k = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}$~J/K is the Boltzmann constant, $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ is the system noise temperature in kelvin, $R_b$ is the data rate in bits per second, $\lambda = c/f$ is the wavelength, and $R$ is the slant range. The factor $L_{\mathrm{o}} \geq 1$ collects the losses other than free-space spreading: atmospheric, pointing, polarization and implementation losses. Expressed in decibels, \eqref{eq:ebn0} becomes
\begin{gather}
\eqname{Link Budget in Decibels}\\
\begin{split}
\left[\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right]_{\mathrm{dB}} &= \mathrm{EIRP} + \left[\frac{G}{T}\right]_{r} - L_{\mathrm{FS}} \\
&\quad - L_{\mathrm{o}} - k_{\mathrm{dB}} - [R_b]_{\mathrm{dB}},
\end{split}
\label{eq:linkdb}
\end{gather}
where all quantities are in decibels: $\mathrm{EIRP} = P_t + G_t$ in dBW, $[G/T]_r = G_r - 10\log_{10} T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ in dB/K, $L_{\mathrm{FS}} = 20\log_{10}(4\pi R f / c)$ and $L_{\mathrm{o}}$ are positive losses in dB, and $k_{\mathrm{dB}} = -228.6$~dBW/K/Hz. Equation~\eqref{eq:linkdb} is the form used in the design control tables of deep-space practice, which list every gain and loss of a link line by line~\cite{yuen_descanso,dsn_810_005}.



\subsection{Link Margin}

The delivered $E_b/N_0$ obtained from \eqref{eq:ebn0} is compared against the value required by the chosen modulation and coding scheme, giving the margin
\begin{gather}
\eqname{Link Margin}\\
M = \left[\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right]_{\mathrm{del}} - \left[\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right]_{\mathrm{req}},
\label{eq:margin}
\end{gather}
in which the implementation loss is carried inside $L_{\mathrm{o}}$ and is therefore not subtracted a second time. A link is considered closed, meaning that it delivers its data rate at the required error rate, when $M > 0$. Deep-space practice specifies the margin statistically: the mean margin must exceed a multiple of its standard deviation, typically two standard deviations for telemetry and three for commands~\cite{yuen_descanso}. In this tutorial a fixed threshold of \SI{3}{dB} is used as a simplified substitute for that criterion.



\subsection{The Aperture-Range Scaling Law}

Solving \eqref{eq:ebn0} for the aperture product, the product of the transmit and receive antenna diameters, yields
\begin{gather}
\eqname{Aperture-Range Scaling Law}\\
D_t D_r = \frac{4 R \lambda}{\pi \eta} \sqrt{\frac{k T_{\mathrm{sys}} R_b L_{\mathrm{o}} \left(E_b/N_0\right)_{\mathrm{req}}}{P_t}}.
\label{eq:aperture}
\end{gather}
Equation~\eqref{eq:aperture} is the organizing equation of this paper. Its three factors are the three parts of the design problem. The first, $4R/(\pi\eta)$, is geometry (with the aperture efficiency $\eta$), fixed by the orbit or by the Earth--Moon distance (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}). The second, $\lambda$, is the frequency axis (Section~\ref{sec:frequency}). The third, the square root, is hardware and demand; $P_t$ and $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ in it are themselves functions of frequency through Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. Different combinations of its parameters can meet the same link requirement. Of these parameters, only the slant range is fixed by a single earlier decision: the choice of relay orbit. Coverage and stationkeeping, the propellant spent to hold the orbit, constrain that choice from outside the link budget, so the orbit fixes the range and, through \eqref{eq:aperture}, the aperture product. The slant range enters linearly, so that doubling the range doubles the required aperture product. The data rate enters as $\sqrt{R_b}$, so that quadrupling the rate doubles the required aperture product. The wavelength enters linearly, so that raising the carrier frequency reduces the required aperture but narrows the beamwidth, shifting the constraint from the radio-frequency subsystem to the pointing subsystem.
The law holds for $D_t, D_r \gg \lambda$; where it returns an aperture smaller than the wavelength the link is not aperture-limited.

\subsection{A Canonical Budget: The Ka-Band Trunk}
\label{sec:trunk}


As a worked example, we evaluate one budget in full: the trunk link from a relay satellite at lunar distance to a \SI{34}{m} DSN ground station at Ka-band. We choose this case because the ground-station aperture is large enough to offset the path loss over \SI{384400}{km}. The trunk therefore serves as a reference for reading the parametric results below. Table~\ref{tab:budget} lists the parameters.

The antenna gains follow from \eqref{eq:gain} as $G_t = \SI{44.0}{dBi}$ and $G_r = \SI{77.1}{dBi}$, the latter consistent with the DSN 34-m beam-waveguide handbook value of \SI{77.2}{dBi} at \SI{26.25}{GHz}~\cite{dsn_34m_bwg}. The system noise temperature is the handbook value for the \SI{26}{GHz} receive chain. The free-space path loss follows from \eqref{eq:fspl} as
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{FS}} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \cdot 3.844\times10^{8} \cdot 26\times10^{9}}{2.998\times10^{8}}\right) = \SI{232.4}{dB}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into \eqref{eq:linkdb} gives a delivered ratio of
\begin{align}
\left[\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right]_{\mathrm{dB}} &= (16 + 44.0) + (77.1 - 16.4) \nonumber \\
&\quad - 232.4 - 2 + 228.6 - 80 = \SI{34.9}{dB},
\end{align}
and hence a margin of $M = 34.9 - 10 = \SI{24.9}{dB}$ by \eqref{eq:margin}.

\begin{remark}[Aperture Asymmetry on the Trunk Link]
\label{rem:asymmetry}
The \SI{33}{dB} gain difference between the \SI{34}{m} ground antenna and the \SI{0.75}{m} relay antenna allows the Earth--Moon trunk to close. The ground station, unlike the spacecraft, is unconstrained in mass and power, so most of the gain required over the \SI{384400}{km} range is provided at the Earth end. Any link in which both apertures are constrained, such as the inter-satellite case of Section~\ref{sec:link4}, lacks this asymmetry and requires larger apertures or higher power to close.
\end{remark}

The dominant term is the \SI{232.4}{dB} path loss, which is offset mainly by the \SI{77.1}{dBi} receive gain of the DSN aperture. Reducing the ground aperture from \SI{34}{m} to \SI{3.4}{m} would remove \SI{20}{dB} of receive gain and reduce the margin to \SI{4.9}{dB}, which exceeds the \SI{3}{dB} threshold by only \SI{1.9}{dB} before weather losses.

Atmospheric attenuation at the ground station, the absorption of the signal by gases, clouds and rain, reduces the margin further. The DSN link design handbook~\cite{dsn_810_005} gives the yearly attenuation at \SI{26}{GHz} for the three complexes. At 99~percent weather availability, that is, for the attenuation exceeded during only 1~percent of the year, and \SI{20}{\degree} elevation, it is approximately \SI{1.8}{}, \SI{3.5}{} and \SI{3.3}{dB} at Goldstone, Canberra and Madrid. The handbook does not extend to 99.9~percent; for that percentile ITU-R P.618~\cite{itu_p618} predicts approximately \SI{8.6}{}, \SI{19.1}{} and \SI{10.7}{dB} at \SI{20}{\degree} elevation. The attenuating atmosphere also radiates, and this sky noise raises the system noise temperature. With this sky-noise increase and the elevation dependence of the antenna gain and noise temperature~\cite{dsn_34m_bwg} included, the margin at \SI{20}{\degree} elevation and 99.9~percent availability is approximately \SI{8.7}{}, \SI{-2.3}{} and \SI{6.4}{dB} at Goldstone, Canberra and Madrid. The link therefore closes at 99.9~percent availability at Goldstone and Madrid, whereas Canberra requires rate adaptation or site diversity. Corresponding rain-attenuation models are discussed in~\cite{islam_ka_band}.

The handbook also models gain and system temperature against elevation for the \SI{34}{m} beam-waveguide antennas~\cite{dsn_34m_bwg}. With these models, the same budget gives \SI{24.7}{dB} at zenith in clear weather, close to the \SI{24.9}{dB} above. With the clear-sky zenith atmosphere of Canberra it gives \SI{24.6}{dB}, the value in Appendix~\ref{app:dct}. A further term is specific to the Moon. When the relay is seen against the lunar disk, the \SI{0.021}{\degree} station beam lies wholly on the \SI{0.5}{\degree} Moon, whose warm surface adds about \SI{150}{K} to the system temperature~\cite{dsn_810_005}. At \SI{20}{\degree} elevation and 90~percent weather, this lunar noise lowers the margin from \SI{20.6}{} to \SI{16.6}{dB}. Every case down to \SI{10}{\degree} elevation at 99~percent still supports more than \SI{0.5}{Gbps} with a \SI{3}{dB} margin, so the trunk rate is bounded by the \SI{1.5}{GHz} allocation and by the ground receiver.

The parameters of Table~\ref{tab:budget} are close to those of a flown link. LRO has returned its science data since 2009 over a \SI{25.65}{GHz} downlink from a \SI{40}{W} transmitter and a \SI{0.75}{m} antenna. It sends \SI{100}{Mbps} in two orthogonal \SI{50}{Mbps} channels to the \SI{18}{m} WS1 antenna at White Sands~\cite{lro_ka_analysis}. That antenna has a G/T of \SI{46.98}{dB/K} at \SI{10}{\degree} elevation in clear sky~\cite{nen_ug_rev4}. The \SI{0.04}{\degree} station beam also lies on the lunar disk, whose brightness temperature of up to \SI{275}{K}~\cite{lro_ka_analysis} adds an estimated \SI{73}{}--\SI{173}{K}. Evaluating \eqref{eq:linkdb} with these values, $L_{\mathrm{o}} = \SI{2}{dB}$ and slant ranges of about \SI{354000}{}--\SI{411000}{km} gives $E_b/N_0$ of \SI{17.8}{}--\SI{20.5}{dB} at \SI{100}{Mbps}. The predicted spread of \SI{2.7}{dB} between favorable and adverse conditions is close to the spread between the project's favorable and adverse predictions. More than 99~percent of the one-second measurements at White Sands lie within \SI{3}{dB} below the adverse prediction~\cite{lro_ka_analysis}. The flown rate is set by the channel bandwidth and the ground receiver, and most of this margin remains unused. The budget above predicts this bandwidth-limited regime for the trunk.

Fig.~\ref{fig:aperture} shows how large the transmit aperture must be to hold a \SI{3}{dB} margin at Ka-band when the receiver is a \SI{0.5}{m} relay antenna. \subsubsection{Gain and Beamwidth Versus Aperture Diameter}
Fig.~\ref{fig:gainbw} plots the gain (left axis) and the half-power beamwidth (right axis) of a Ka-band aperture against its diameter, for $\eta = 0.6$. The half-power beamwidth is the angular width of the main beam over which the aperture delivers at least half its peak power. A \SI{0.75}{m} antenna provides approximately \SI{44}{dBi} of gain with a beamwidth of approximately \SI{1.1}{\degree}, whereas a \SI{34}{m} DSN antenna provides approximately \SI{77}{dBi} with the handbook beamwidth of \SI{0.021}{\degree}. The DSN holds this narrow beam on target with ground-based pointing equipment that a spacecraft or lander lacks. Conversely, a \SI{0.1}{m} lander antenna has a beamwidth near \SI{8}{\degree}, which relaxes the pointing requirement but yields only approximately \SI{26}{dBi}. As the diameter grows, the gain rises and the beamwidth shrinks, so the two curves diverge monotonically. This divergence is the coupling noted in Section~\ref{sec:gain}: a higher antenna gain imposes a tighter pointing requirement on the platform.

\subsection{The Six Canonical Links}
\label{sec:sixlinks}
A lunar mission uses six canonical links, from the short surface hop to the trunk to Earth. Table~\ref{tab:links} summarizes them, numbered as in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}; each is an instance of \eqref{eq:aperture} evaluated at a different slant range. Four of the six are limited by geometry, whether through terrain, range, aperture or pointing; link~5 is limited by weather and scheduling, and link~6 by its allocation. The values in Table~\ref{tab:links} are indicative, and the bands follow the band plan of~\cite{sfcg_rec32}; Section~\ref{sec:ladder} gives the alternatives for links~3 and~4. Each rate holds a \SI{3}{dB} margin over uncoded QPSK at the ends of its range interval; for link~4 the two points are the \SI{16800}{km} mean LCRNS crosslink range and \SI{60000}{km}. Links~2 to~4 assume $P_t = \SI{10}{W}$, a \SI{500}{K} receiver on the relay and $L_{\mathrm{o}} = \SI{2}{dB}$. On the trunk, link~5 from the relay to Earth, the \SI{34}{m} ground aperture is large enough that range no longer sets the limit.

\subsubsection{Link 1: Surface Access}
Link~1 in Table~\ref{tab:links} is the surface access link, connecting a rover, lander or suited astronaut to a local relay at \SI{1}{}--\SI{10}{km}. The dominant constraint is terrain blockage. In the \SI{2503.5}{}--\SI{2655}{MHz} band that the lunar band plan assigns to surface links~\cite{sfcg_rec32}, the free-space loss over \SI{10}{km} at the band center is \SI{120.7}{dB}. Take $T_{\mathrm{sys}} = \SI{290}{K}$, $L_{\mathrm{o}} = \SI{2}{dB}$ and the uncoded requirement of Table~\ref{tab:budget}. A \SI{1}{W} (\SI{0}{dBW}) transmitter with an omnidirectional (\SI{0}{dBi}) antenna, received by a \SI{3}{dBi} antenna, then closes the link at \SI{1}{Mbps} with a margin of \SI{14.3}{dB} (\SI{14.6}{}--\SI{14.0}{dB} across the band). With a \SI{3}{dB} margin, the same link supports \SI{13}{Mbps}.

The cell-planning consequences of terrain, and the 3GPP study that preceded the first surface network, are treated in Section~\ref{sec:surface}.

\subsubsection{Link 2: Surface-to-Relay}
Link~2 in Table~\ref{tab:links} joins a lander or rover carrying a pointable \SI{0.5}{m} S-band dish to the relay, with a dish of the same size at the relay. With the assumptions above, it closes \SI{1}{Mbps} with a \SI{3}{dB} margin only to about \SI{5900}{km}. It carries \SI{100}{kbps} with \SI{3.5}{dB} of margin at the \SI{17400}{km} LCRNS apolune (Appendix~\ref{app:dct}) and falls to about \SI{7}{kbps} at the \SI{70000}{km} apolune of an NRHO. The link is therefore limited by the aperture that a surface terminal can carry. A rate of \SI{20}{Mbps} corresponds approximately to a \SI{20}{MHz} QPSK carrier. A handset-class user radiating \SI{0.2}{W} from an omnidirectional antenna falls about \SI{55}{dB} short of this rate at \SI{15000}{}--\SI{17400}{km}, even with the \SI{0.5}{m} dish at the relay. Suited astronauts and small rovers therefore reach the relay through a surface gateway, over links~1 and~3.

\subsubsection{Link 3: Surface-Gateway Backhaul}
Link~3 in Table~\ref{tab:links} is the backhaul from a surface gateway or habitat to an orbital relay. This link operates at Ka-band (\SI{26}{GHz}) with directional antennas on both ends, and the dominant constraint is the product of aperture and pointing accuracy. For a south-polar gateway the relevant range is the relay's apolune, where it dwells over the pole. Table~\ref{tab:links} lists a pair of \SI{0.5}{m} antennas for this link. With the relay assumptions above and $R_b = \SI{10}{Mbps}$, the pair closes with \SI{5.0}{dB} of margin at the \SI{17400}{km} apolune of the LCRNS reference ELFO~\cite{lcrns_ref_const}. With a \SI{3}{dB} margin the same pair supports \SI{16}{Mbps} there and \SI{63}{Mbps} at the \SI{8700}{km} apolune of the smaller ELFO, comparable to the \SI{1}{}--\SI{50}{Mbps} Ka-band user rates specified for LCRNS~\cite{nasa_lunar_relay_srd}. At the \SI{69400}{km} apolune of the NRHO the margin falls to \SI{-7.0}{dB}. The NRHO range penalty of \SI{12}{dB} is recovered either by a sixteen-fold reduction in data rate or by enlarging both antennas to approximately \SI{0.9}{m} for a \SI{3}{dB} margin. The required aperture product grows with the fourfold range ratio, and each diameter grows with its square root. The backhaul is thus coupled to orbit selection: an NRHO relay requires approximately twice the antenna diameter at both ends compared with an ELFO relay. The larger antennas narrow the Ka-band beam from \SI{1.6}{\degree} to approximately \SI{0.9}{\degree}. They also tighten the pointing requirement, taken as the error that costs \SI{0.5}{dB} at one end, from \SI{0.33}{\degree} to \SI{0.18}{\degree}.




\subsubsection{Link 4: Inter-Satellite, Radio and Optical}
\label{sec:link4}
Link~4 connects two relay satellites in lunar orbit. With \SI{1}{m} dishes at both ends and the same relay assumptions, its radio-frequency version supports about \SI{40}{Mbps} at zero margin at \SI{60000}{km}. An optical version closes with positive margin at comparable aperture sizes. Take $\lambda = \SI{1550}{nm}$, $P_t = \SI{+5}{dBW}$ (\SI{3}{W}), $D_t = D_r = \SI{10}{cm}$, $\eta = 0.5$, $R_b = \SI{1}{Gbps}$ and differential phase-shift keying (DPSK) with an optically preamplified receiver. The telescope gain is then \SI{103.1}{dB} at each terminal, and the free-space loss is \SI{293.7}{dB}. For a Gaussian beam the pointing loss is approximately $\exp(-G_t\theta_e^2)$ for a pointing error $\theta_e$, which amounts to \SI{0.8}{dB} at $\theta_e = \SI{3}{\micro\radian}$ against a beamwidth of $1.22\lambda/D = \SI{18.9}{\micro\radian}$. The received power is then $5 + 103.1 + 103.1 - 293.7 - 0.8 = \SI{-83.3}{dBW}$. An uncoded preamplified DPSK receiver requires approximately 25~photons per bit~\cite{caplan_laser_comm}, or \SI{-84.9}{dBW} at \SI{1}{Gbps}, so the margin is \SI{1.6}{dB} before any coding gain.

The budget is most sensitive to the pointing allowance. LLCD stabilized its line of sight to better than \SI{2.5}{\micro\radian} (Section~\ref{sec:assets}). Doubling the pointing error to \SI{5}{\micro\radian} raises the pointing loss to approximately \SI{2.2}{dB} and reduces the margin to approximately \SI{0.2}{dB}. Because the loss grows with the square of the error, the link no longer closes at \SI{6}{\micro\radian}. Flight results supporting these parameters include optical downlinks from lunar and low-Earth-orbit missions demonstrating \SI{200}{}--\SI{260}{Mbps} with apertures of \SI{10}{}--\SI{22}{cm}~\cite{nasa_o2o,tbird_optical}, and proposed Moon-to-Earth links targeting \SI{2.5}{Gbps}~\cite{jaxa_optical_relay}. To the authors' knowledge, no inter-satellite optical link at cislunar range has been demonstrated in flight. The parameters above are consistent with the design of the Inter-Spacecraft Optical Communicator (ISOC), which targets gigabit connectivity among lunar smallsats with a multi-telescope omnidirectional terminal~\cite{isoc_2026}.



\subsubsection{Link 5: Trunk to Earth}
Link~5 is the canonical budget of Section~\ref{sec:trunk}: \SI{24.7}{dB} of margin at \SI{100}{Mbps} at zenith in clear weather with the handbook models, and more than \SI{0.5}{Gbps} at a \SI{3}{dB} margin in every case down to \SI{10}{\degree} elevation at 99~percent availability. Its rate is bounded by the \SI{1.5}{GHz} allocation and the ground receiver, and the volume it delivers is bounded by station time and weather; the chain example below assumes one \SI{8}{h} pass per day.

\subsubsection{Link 6: Direct-to-Earth}
Link~6 in Table~\ref{tab:links} is the direct-to-Earth link from a near-side lander, the link used by the near-side landers of Section~\ref{sec:assets}. Take a \SI{0.6}{m} steerable antenna radiating \SI{20}{W} at \SI{8.45}{GHz} toward a \SI{34}{m} Deep Space Network antenna with the handbook X-band gain of \SI{68.3}{dBi} and zenith system temperature of \SI{21.25}{K}~\cite{dsn_34m_bwg}, $\eta = 0.6$ and $L_{\mathrm{o}} = \SI{2}{dB}$. The lander gain is \SI{32.3}{dBi}, the free-space loss over \SI{384400}{km} is \SI{222.7}{dB}, and the carrier-to-noise density is $13 + 32.3 + 68.3 - 222.7 - 2 - 13.3 + 228.6 = \SI{104.2}{dB\,Hz}$, which under the uncoded quadrature phase-shift keying requirement of Table~\ref{tab:budget} supports more than \SI{1}{Gbps}. The link is therefore limited by the \SI{50}{MHz} of the \SI{8450}{}--\SI{8500}{MHz} space research allocation, which caps it near \SI{100}{Mbps} at two bits per symbol. It is also limited by geometry. The lander must have Earth above its local horizon; at a south-polar site Earth lies a few degrees above the horizon, where a crater rim or the lander's own structure can block it. The \SI{4.1}{\degree} beam of a \SI{0.6}{m} X-band antenna must also follow the \SI{\pm 7}{\degree} libration of Earth. The scaling of \eqref{eq:aperture} that favors Ka-band on the trunk applies here as well. Moving to Ka-band narrows the beam to \SI{1.3}{\degree} and raises the available bandwidth from \SI{50}{MHz} to \SI{1.5}{GHz}. For this reason, the occupied-bandwidth rule of the SFCG directs wide links to Ka-band (Section~\ref{sec:assets}). Blue Ghost Mission~1 (March 2025) returned \SI{6}{Mbps} on average and \SI{10}{Mbps} at peak over its X-band antenna~\cite{nssdca_bgm1}, below both the power limit and the allocation limit.

\subsubsection{Links 3 and 4 Above Ka-Band}
Table~\ref{tab:links} sizes links~3 and~4 at Ka-band because that is the band of the SFCG plan. Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} gives their rates across the ladder at \SI{100}{W} of DC power, which yields about \SI{40}{W} of RF at Ka-band against the \SI{10}{W} of Table~\ref{tab:links}, with no margin. At \SI{60000}{km} the inter-satellite link returns about \SI{0.2}{Gbps} at Ka-band and \SI{0.5}{}--\SI{0.7}{Gbps} from E-band to about \SI{240}{GHz} with the laboratory tubes of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, while the beam narrows from \SI{0.8}{\degree} to \SI{0.15}{\degree} at D-band. The backhaul rate scales in the same way across the ladder. The design-control table of Appendix~\ref{app:dct} gives both links at Ka-, E- and D-band side by side.

\subsection{A Chain-Level Example}
Chained together, the six links form a network. Table~\ref{tab:chain} follows one path through it, combining links~1, 3 and~5 from a rover through a surface gateway and an ELFO relay to a \SI{34}{m} station. Each hop is sized with the assumptions of Table~\ref{tab:links}. Real-time traffic accumulates delay hop by hop to a one-way total of \SI{2.35}{\second}, of which \SI{1.34}{\second} is propagation, so the relay processing allowance of the LCRNS requirements~\cite{nasa_lunar_relay_srd} is comparable to the light time to Earth. Throughput accumulates differently, because each hop is available only for its contact duty cycle (Section~\ref{sec:admissibility}). With the trunk scheduled for one \SI{8}{h} pass per day and storage at the gateway and at the relay, the chain delivers on average the \SI{8.3}{Mbps} of the backhaul, enough for eight rovers at \SI{1}{Mbps}. The storage requirement follows from the contact gaps: each \SI{1}{Mbps} rover stream needs \SI{2.5}{GB} at the gateway to cover the largest relay gap, \SI{5.6}{h}, and the relay needs \SI{60}{GB} to hold \SI{16}{h} of backhaul traffic between passes, which it then drains at a quarter of the trunk rate. Without storage, a real-time stream requires all three hops to be in view simultaneously; if the contacts are independent this occurs 28~percent of the time. The limiting hop of the chain is the backhaul, whereas the trunk, which dominates the delay, has the largest margin and the most unused rate.

\section{The Surface Segment: Coverage, Capacity and Mobility}
\label{sec:surface}
Sections~\ref{sec:geometry} to~\ref{sec:budgets} treated each link in isolation; the following sections treat links that are shared, chained and interrupted. The surface is treated first because terrestrial cellular practice transfers there most directly, so the departures imposed by lunar conditions are easiest to identify. The surface segment of Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} has two parts: the access link between users and a gateway, and the mesh among users. Section~\ref{sec:demand} quantified the demand it serves. The subsections below show how terrain sets coverage, how the few users share the cell and the relay, why handover is triggered by predicted visibility, and what the first link-level results are.

\subsection{Terrain-Limited Coverage}
\label{sec:coverage}
On the Moon, coverage depends on antenna height and node placement more than on transmit power. Link~1 of Table~\ref{tab:links} closes with \SI{14}{dB} of margin over \SI{10}{km} at \SI{1}{Mbps} with omnidirectional antennas; its coverage is limited by visibility. The first limit is the horizon of Section~\ref{sec:geometry}, and the second is the terrain within it. A line-of-sight path over regolith is served at close to the free-space level, modulated by two-ray interference between the direct ray and the ground reflection. A path behind a ridge is served, if at all, through diffraction over one or more edges, and the diffraction loss grows with frequency. Reference~\cite{baena2027surface} quantifies both limits over a \SI{10}{km} south-polar tile at Connecting Ridge. The study uses the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA) elevation model, a two-ray model for line-of-sight paths and multi-edge diffraction for shadowed ones. A single \SI{30}{m} mast on the highest point of the tile serves 35.1~percent of it at UHF, 18.7~percent at S-band and 12.4~percent at Ka-band. About 12~percent of the tile is in line of sight, whereas a free-space (Friis) budget without terrain would serve 100~percent. At the Shackleton rim tile, the served fractions are 42.8, 30.2 and 22.8~percent.

Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}(a) compares the two tiles and shows the line-of-sight core of Connecting Ridge. Panels~(b) to~(d) vary one parameter at a time at S-band on Connecting Ridge; in every case, a Friis budget without terrain would serve the whole tile. Raising the mast from 10 to \SI{100}{m} raises the S-band served fraction from 4.8 to 44.3~percent, and three nodes on high points serve 33.9~percent. Transmit power has a smaller effect: raising the EIRP from the \SI{23}{dBm} of a suit radio to the \SI{53}{dBm} of a base station, at a \SI{10}{m} mast, raises the fraction only from 12 to 19~percent. The S-band margin over the tile has a bimodal distribution: the served locations have a median margin of \SI{+34}{dB}, with the line-of-sight peak near \SI{+38}{dB}, and the unserved locations a median of \SI{-59}{dB}. Coverage therefore consists of a line-of-sight core with large margin and a diffraction fringe that shrinks with frequency.

\begin{remark}[Mast Height and Transmit Power]
A surface link budget is best spent on antenna height and node placement before transmit power, and the handover overlap is best sized from the terrain map.
\end{remark}

Along a \SI{1.79}{km} rover traverse at the Shackleton rim, 178 of 179 waypoints are in line of sight. At these waypoints the channel varies only through two-ray interference, between \SI{-12.6}{} and \SI{+5.2}{dB} relative to free space. At the single waypoint where a small ridge blocks the path, the loss rises by \SI{12.5}{dB} within one \SI{10}{m} step; a prediction from the elevation model can anticipate this jump.

\subsection{Cells and Node Density}
\label{sec:cells}
In its design approach, the access link resembles a terrestrial small cell. The study that preceded the IM-2 surface cellular system (Section~\ref{sec:assets}) modeled an LTE network-in-a-box, a base station and core network in a single unit, mounted on a lander at a height of \SI{15}{} or \SI{30}{m} with \SI{5}{dBi} antennas. It found that links can be supported up to \SI{10}{km}, with a peak uplink rate near \SI{70}{Mbps} on a \SI{20}{MHz} carrier~\cite{nokia_lunar_3gpp}. In a lunar cell, unlike a terrestrial small cell, the boundary follows terrain rather than a contour of constant received power. Cell planning therefore requires a ray tracer operating on a digital elevation model~\cite{azami_terrain_aware,zhu_channel_ray}, and node density is set by terrain rather than by co-channel interference. The ITU-R Irregular Lunar Model~\cite{itu_p2170} provides the standardized propagation prediction, but as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:measurement} it has not been validated against in-situ measurement.

\subsection{Capacity and Multiple Access}
\label{sec:admissibility}
Within the cell, users share the carrier as they do on Earth, through orthogonal frequency-division multiple access (OFDMA) under a local scheduler. With few terminals and no interference from neighboring cells, the cell is not interference-limited in the terrestrial sense. The link toward the relay also serves few terminals and is noise-limited, and access to it is a scheduling problem over visibility windows (Table~\ref{tab:rosetta_radio}). The governing quantity is therefore the contact duty cycle, the fraction of time the relay is in view and scheduled. Averaged over the contact plan, the throughput a relay delivers to a site is the link rate times this duty cycle. Reference~\cite{baena2027lunabridge} quantifies the duty cycle for a single LCRNS relay over Malapert Peak. With the 90-day plan of Fig.~\ref{fig:dutycycle}, the relay is in view 83.4~percent of the time; the median gap between contacts is \SI{4.9}{h} and the largest \SI{5.6}{h}. The effective backhaul $C_{\mathrm{eff}}$, the link rate times the contact duty cycle, is about \SI{8.3}{Mbps}. Two conditions determine the delivery of a traffic class. A bundle, the data unit that delay-tolerant networking stores and forwards, survives a gap only if its lifetime exceeds the gap. A class whose lifetime is shorter than the largest gap is therefore gap-limited: an alert with a real-time lifetime of \SI{300}{s} delivers only the duty cycle, 0.83, at any link rate, buffer size or scheduling policy. A class whose lifetime exceeds the largest gap reaches full delivery if its rate fits the effective backhaul. Otherwise it is capacity-limited at $C_{\mathrm{eff}}/R$, so that a \SI{12}{Mbps} video feed delivers 0.69 for any bundle lifetime.

\begin{remark}[Bundle Lifetime and Contact Gaps]
The bundle lifetime of each traffic class should be set from the largest gap of the contact plan. A lifetime set from the latency target alone loses every bundle generated during a gap.
\end{remark}

Fig.~\ref{fig:dutycycle} draws both tests on the plane of bundle lifetime and offered rate, for the plan with a \SI{5}{\degree} elevation mask. Below the largest gap a class is gap-limited, and above $C_{\mathrm{eff}}$ it is capacity-limited. The dashed contours give the delivery $P = \min(P_{\mathrm{gap}}(L), C_{\mathrm{eff}}/R)$. The dots are the traffic classes defined in~\cite{baena2027lunabridge}, labeled with their delivery where it falls below 1. The scheduling policy matters only when contacts are congested; under nominal coverage first-in-first-out, strict priority and weighted fair queuing deliver the same set~\cite{baena2027lunabridge}. 

The published waveform studies do not address capacity. Jia \emph{et al.}~\cite{jia_waveform} recommend OFDMA on the uplink and SC-FDMA on the downlink, the reverse of the LTE assignment, which gives a direct-link range near \SI{6}{km}. That analysis reports peak-to-average power ratio and bit error rate but not aggregate throughput or simultaneous user counts. The BEACON concept~\cite{beacon_demo} proposes a mast of approximately \SI{15}{m} combining power beaming with a 3GPP relay for users within approximately \SI{10}{km}; it describes infrastructure and reports no capacity results.

\subsection{Earth-Origin Emissions}
The near side is illuminated by every terrestrial emitter whose signal passes the ionosphere, and the surface bands at \SI{2.4}{}--\SI{2.69}{GHz} overlap terrestrial industrial, scientific and medical and International Mobile Telecommunications (IMT) bands. The ROLSES-1 surface instrument recorded terrestrial shortwave transmissions~\cite{hibbard_rolses2025} (Section~\ref{sec:measurement}); the interference environment at \SI{2.4}{GHz} has not been measured in situ. For communications, the interference expected from Earth is small. Over the \SI{384400}{km} path the free-space loss at \SI{2.4}{GHz} is about \SI{212}{dB}, so a terrestrial transmitter with an EIRP of \SI{60}{dBW} reaches an isotropic lunar antenna at about \SI{-152}{dBW}. That level lies below the thermal noise of a \SI{1}{MHz} receiver at \SI{290}{K}, about \SI{-144}{dBW}. Only transmitters that point high-gain antennas at the Moon, such as deep-space uplinks and planetary radars, exceed it. Radio astronomy instruments are orders of magnitude more sensitive than communications receivers, so Earth-origin emissions matter mainly for near-side science. The far side is shielded from these terrestrial emissions, which is one of the reasons it is sought for radio astronomy.

\subsection{Mobility in Binary Coverage}
\label{sec:mobility}

Two kinds of signal variation matter for mobility. Shadowing is the slow change of received power when an obstacle blocks the path; fading is the fast change caused by multipath. On Earth both are random. Buildings, vehicles and foliage make shadowing a log-normal process, and multipath makes fading follow Rayleigh or Rician statistics. Handover algorithms therefore filter the measured signal and trigger when its average falls below a threshold. On the Moon, shadowing comes from terrain alone, and no atmospheric refraction or scattering fills the shadow. The loss can therefore rise within a few meters of travel: along the Shackleton traverse of Section~\ref{sec:coverage}, it rises by \SI{12.5}{dB} within one \SI{10}{m} step. A rover traveling at \SI{1}{}--\SI{5}{m/s} loses the link in under \SI{1}{\second} when the obstructing feature lies within \SI{100}{m}~\cite{azami_terrain_aware}. A filtered signal level gives little warning of such a loss, so the terrestrial trigger loses its lead time.

Fading on lunar line-of-sight paths is mild and also predictable. The channel varies only through two-ray interference between the direct ray and the ground reflection, within the range given in Section~\ref{sec:coverage}. Both effects follow from mapped terrain and known relay ephemerides, so visibility and the two-ray pattern along a planned traverse can be computed in advance. The handover trigger must therefore be computed from predicted visibility. Handover must be initiated while the serving link is still at full strength. Early initiation requires a region of overlap with a second visible relay or with a surface relay sited beyond the obstruction. The extent of that overlap must be sized from the terrain instead of a statistical shadowing margin. Where the deployment provides no overlap the rover buffers traffic and forwards it at the next contact, which is the delay-tolerant model~\cite{rfc9171,rfc4838}. Where the terrain removes the second link that overlap would require, a passive reflector placed at the obstructing edge can in principle provide one, an idea taken up in Section~\ref{sec:open}.




\subsection{Link-Level Performance Over the Lunar Channel}
\label{sec:linklevel}
Baena \emph{et al.}~\cite{baena2027surface} run a 5G New Radio uplink simulation, a physical uplink shared channel (PUSCH) of one layer over \SI{18.4}{MHz} with two receive antennas, with the channels exported from the ray-traced terrain. The lunar line-of-sight channel is frequency-flat, because the two-ray excess delay is far shorter than the cyclic prefix, the guard interval that absorbs echoes in each symbol. This channel reaches a block error rate of 10~percent about \SI{5}{dB} below the frequency-selective terrestrial reference channel. From \SI{9.5}{dB} upward it has no block errors; there a single modulation and coding scheme saturates at \SI{28}{Mbps}, and link adaptation over eight schemes reaches \SI{102}{Mbps}. Fig.~\ref{fig:bler} places these schemes against the Shannon bound of the same \SI{18.4}{MHz}: the link-adaptation envelope delivers \SI{31}{Mbps} at \SI{10}{dB} and \SI{65}{Mbps} at \SI{18}{dB}, between half and 60~percent of the bound. The gap is due to the reference-signal, coding and modulation overhead of the 3GPP waveform and to a practical receiver. A non-line-of-sight stress case with two taps behaves like the terrestrial reference, whereas the ray-traced diffracted lunar links have a single tap. A served lunar link therefore requires a lower SNR than a terrestrial one at the same block error rate, so coverage is limited by visibility; this is the link-level counterpart of the binary coverage above. Section~\ref{sec:measurement} describes how the same channels are reproduced on radio hardware.

\section{The Space Segment: Constellations and the Relay}
\label{sec:space}
The backhaul throughput of a surface cell is limited by the fraction of time a relay is in view. The navigation requirement sets the number of relays, as the coverage analysis below shows. Without Earth GNSS, the relays must also provide position and time (Section~\ref{sec:pnt}), and they must carry the payloads that do so.

\subsection{Coverage}
The planned lunar relay constellations are regional, optimized for the south polar region in which the Artemis missions are planned to operate~\cite{lcrns_ref_const,esa_moonlight_csc}. Published coverage analyses indicate that two large-amplitude halo satellites give continuous single-satellite coverage of one polar region (latitudes above \SI{60}{\degree}) and that four approach complete surface coverage~\cite{gao_halo_coverage}. For an eight-satellite ELFO constellation with terrain occlusion at the site, the probability of four or more satellites being visible is 69~percent, rising to 96~percent for a nine-satellite hybrid of ELFO and NRHO~\cite{zhang_elfo_occlusion}.

Communications availability and navigation availability scale differently. Communications requires one visible satellite, whereas a navigation fix requires four in favorable geometry. In a six-satellite ELFO model, four or more satellites are visible at the south pole 99~percent of the time, yet the geometric dilution of precision (GDOP, Section~\ref{sec:dop}) is below 6 only 16~percent of the time~\cite{baweja_dop}. The orbit families sized in~\cite{asok2027space} against one requirement set show the same difference (Fig.~\ref{fig:commnav}). The figure counts the satellites needed for one and for three simultaneous relay paths at the worst point of the cap over 99.9~percent of a \SI{90}{d} propagation, and for navigation at Malapert Peak. The ELFO is the LCRNS reference orbit, the circular orbits are at \SI{2500}{km} (\SI{86}{\degree}) and \SI{1000}{km} (polar), and for the three-plane ELFO the one-path count shown is the smallest tested. The ELFO and the NRHO keep one relay path at the worst point of the cap with two satellites and three paths with four. Navigation with a PDOP of at most 6 at 99.9~percent needs 13 ELFO satellites in three planes over 21 days and 18 over the full 90-day ballistic arc. A circular constellation in a single plane falls short of that navigation availability at the pole at every size tested in~\cite{asok2027space}, because its satellites lie close to one great circle as seen from the site. In three planes it needs 18.

\begin{remark}[Constellation Size for Navigation]
A polar relay constellation should be sized for navigation first and checked for communications afterward. Sizing it for communications first undersizes it by a factor of six or more.
\end{remark}

A global lunar constellation, one that serves all longitudes and latitudes, requires a different architecture from the regional designs above. For radio-frequency links, Cheung and Lee~\cite{cho_relay_coverage} compare a three-orbiter constellation in polar and equatorial orbits with single relays in an Earth--Moon L2 Lissajous orbit and in a distant retrograde orbit. For optical links, they consider a multi-hop architecture through an L2 relay and geosynchronous relay satellites. The IOAG lunar communications architecture study~\cite{ioag_lunar_arch} proposes a relay constellation of three to five orbiters in \SI{12}{hour} orbits: ELFOs with apolune over the north and south poles and a circular equatorial orbit. The constellation can be built up incrementally, south pole first, then the equator and finally the north pole, and at all latitudes it provides contacts of \SI{5}{}--\SI{7}{hours} with maximum gaps of \SI{1.4}{}--\SI{5.8}{hours}. Regional and global service therefore also differ in deployment sequence. The link budget must accommodate the slant range of each stage, which in that design reaches \SI{5892}{km} to the equatorial orbiter and \SI{9672}{km} to an elliptical one.

\subsection{The Relay Payload}
\label{sec:payload}
Several architectural elements of 3GPP non-terrestrial networks transfer to the relay. The distinction between transparent and regenerative payloads~\cite{3gpp_tr_38821} maps onto the choice between a bent-pipe relay and one performing on-board processing. The decomposition into feeder and service links~\cite{3gpp_tr_38811,3gpp_ntn_overview} maps onto the lunar trunk and the relay-to-surface link. For beam management, 3GPP reuses the terrestrial NR framework as the NTN baseline, and Release~18 extends NR-NTN to Ka-band terminals in bands n510--n512~\cite{3gpp_tr_38821,3gpp_rp240779}; the same framework is a starting point for lunar relay beams. These correspondences are analogies; no 3GPP specification applies them to the lunar case.

A lunar relay satellite carries three payload classes that determine the spacecraft design (Fig.~\ref{fig:payload}). The first is the \emph{bent-pipe transponder}, which receives a signal at one frequency, amplifies it, and retransmits at another, with no on-board processing. Without store-and-forward, it relays data only while the surface user and the Earth ground station are both in view.

The second class is the \emph{regenerative payload}, which demodulates, decodes, and re-encodes the signal on board. A review of the Chinese Queqiao relay satellites trades transparent (bent-pipe) against regenerative modes. Transparent relays accept any signal format but demand more capable ground stations, whereas regenerative relays add coding gain under limited on-board resources; Queqiao itself operated in regenerative mode~\cite{queqiao_relay_review_2021}. A commercial relay designed for store-and-forward is in preparation. SSTL's Lunar Pathfinder, a spacecraft of about \SI{280}{kg} targeted for launch in 2027, is designed to serve lunar users over S-band and UHF links under the CCSDS Proximity-1 protocol. It is designed to connect to Earth over a two-way X-band link and to hold user data in its payload data recorder until the next Earth contact~\cite{davies_pathfinder_2023,sstl_pathfinder_guide,sstl_pathfinder_mission}. In 3GPP terms, a regenerative relay can host the distributed unit of the 5G base station (gNB), with the centralized unit on the ground~\cite{3gpp_tr_38821}.

The LunaNet interoperability specification defines both a frame-level real-time relay service that needs no processing of user data and a Bundle Protocol store-and-forward service, which requires a bundle node, and hence demodulation and decoding, at the relay~\cite{lnis_v5}. The LCRNS requirements exclude a bent-pipe design~\cite{nasa_onboard_lunanet}. NASA's study of onboard processing for LunaNet data services sets out the LCRNS requirements that drive the relay processor. These requirements are Ka-band user rates of up to \SI{50}{Mbps}, simultaneous S-band, Ka-band and PNT services together with direct-to-Earth links, and on-board storage for far-side store-and-forward. The resulting protocol throughput is roughly \SI{100}{Mbps} per relay node~\cite{nasa_onboard_lunanet}. The study compares trunking protocols for the direct-to-Earth link and does not size the hardware.

The third class is the \emph{navigation payload}: a GNSS receiver for weak Earth signals, an ultra-stable oscillator and the generator of the Augmented Forward Signal of Section~\ref{sec:timescale}, feeding a wide-beam S-band antenna that shares the communication power budget~\cite{ansalone_lcrns_time}. Lunar Pathfinder also hosts ESA's NaviMoon GNSS receiver, a \SI{1.4}{kg} payload demonstrating weak-signal GNSS reception from lunar orbit~\cite{esa_navimoon}.

S-band and Ka-band can share one steerable reflector through coaxial or frequency-selective feeds, as on the \SI{0.75}{m} dish of LRO~\cite{lro_ka_analysis}. Relay designs can nevertheless add a separate wide-beam S-band antenna, because surface access and navigation must cover the whole service area while the Ka-band trunk and backhaul need a narrow, steered beam. In the design of the paused Gateway station, the link to Orion is at S-band through the HALO equipment, and optical terminals are identified only as a future upgrade~\cite{gateway_comm}.

The regenerative payload is the hardware prerequisite for every protocol result in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. It adds four costs that a bent pipe avoids: demodulation and decoding at the relay's rates, storage sized for the longest Earth outage of a far-side user, radiation-tolerant compute for the bundle node and the routing, and the power to run them. All four compete with the amplifier for the bus power of Section~\ref{sec:frequency}. Published hardware figures for this cost are scarce. The NASA Glenn COMPASS design of a relay satellite in a \SI{12}{h} elliptical frozen polar orbit serving the south pole gives a detailed public equipment list~\cite{oleson2012compass}. It allots \SI{0.3}{TB} of storage for \SI{24}{h} of relay traffic, an average of \SI{28}{Mbps}. Its processors, data recorders and router account for \SI{48}{kg} of the \SI{563}{kg} dry mass and \SI{157}{W} of the \SI{684}{W} nominal load of the communications and avionics subsystems. The design dates from 2007 and assumed card-based radiation-hardened avionics. Later NASA concept work reduced the relay to a spacecraft of about \SI{225}{kg} wet mass with a \SI{1.1}{kW} solar array, sized for an Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle Secondary Payload Adapter (ESPA) ring. It states on-board storage for store-and-forward as a requirement without giving a capacity~\cite{lcrns_esper2025}. 



\section{Positioning, Navigation and Timing}
\label{sec:pnt}
On Earth, a terminal obtains position and time from a global navigation satellite system (GNSS) almost everywhere, and 3GPP networks use that fix for timing advance and Doppler pre-compensation. At the Moon this support is partial. GNSS signals reach the near side weak and from a narrow cone of directions around Earth, and they do not reach the far side (Section~\ref{sec:gnss}). A lunar user therefore needs a lunar navigation service with three parts: relays that broadcast ranging signals, a geometry good enough to turn ranges into a position, and a time scale that all lunar clocks follow. Each part places a requirement on the rest of the network. Navigation sets the size of the polar constellation (Section~\ref{sec:space}), the time scale enters the protocol timers and the replay protection of the security layer (Sections~\ref{sec:protocol} and~\ref{sec:cross}), and surface operations need position for wayfinding and for recording where samples are taken~\cite{nasa_moonbase_ug2026}.

\subsection{Dilution of Precision}
\label{sec:dop}
A navigation receiver estimates four unknowns, its three position coordinates and its clock offset, from at least four pseudoranges. Each pseudorange carries an error, and the satellite geometry sets how much of that error reaches the solution. The \emph{dilution of precision} (DOP) is this amplification factor: if each pseudorange has an error with standard deviation $\sigma_\rho$, the position error has standard deviation $\mathrm{PDOP}\cdot\sigma_\rho$. The position DOP (PDOP) covers the three position coordinates. The horizontal and vertical DOPs (HDOP and VDOP) split it between the local horizontal plane and the vertical, so that $\mathrm{PDOP}^2=\mathrm{HDOP}^2+\mathrm{VDOP}^2$. The time DOP (TDOP) covers the clock offset, and the geometric DOP (GDOP) combines position and time, $\mathrm{GDOP}^2=\mathrm{PDOP}^2+\mathrm{TDOP}^2$. Satellites spread across the sky give small DOP values. Satellites clustered in one part of the sky give large ones, because their lines of sight are nearly parallel and the receiver cannot separate the coordinates. With a ranging error of \SI{1}{m}, a PDOP of 6, the threshold used in this paper, gives a position error of about \SI{6}{m} (one standard deviation).

At the lunar south pole, the median GDOP varies non-monotonically as ELFO satellites are added. It is 9.6 for five satellites and 21.5 for six, then 7.3 with eight and 8.9 with ten, against a commonly used acceptability threshold of approximately 6. With four satellites, a user never sees the four required for a fix~\cite{baweja_dop}. The degradation is geometric in origin: the satellites occupy similar orbital planes and are therefore clustered in the same region of the sky. The GDOP is the square root of the trace of $(H^{\mathsf{T}}H)^{-1}$, where each row of the observation matrix $H$ contains the unit line-of-sight vector to one satellite, augmented by a unit entry for the receiver clock bias. $H^{\mathsf{T}}H$ is the analog of the channel Gram matrix in MIMO communications. When the rows of $H$ are nearly parallel, the matrix is ill-conditioned, and the position estimate amplifies the ranging error by a factor bounded by the condition number. A MIMO channel with correlated columns yields a low multiplexing gain for the same reason. The lunar south-polar geometry places all visible satellites in a narrow cone, so $H$ is nearly rank-deficient and the condition number is large. Baweja~\cite{baweja_dop} finds that approximately twelve orbital satellites are needed to bring the median below 6 (a median, against the 99.9~percent availability of Fig.~\ref{fig:commnav}). For a user at \SI{-80}{\degree} latitude, three surface beacons reduce it from 16.2 to 1.6, matching a twenty-four-satellite orbital constellation. The surface beacons break the angular degeneracy by adding rows to $H$ with widely differing line-of-sight directions, which is the geometric equivalent of adding well-separated antenna elements to a MIMO array.

\begin{remark}[Surface Beacons and Polar Geometry]
To improve navigation at the pole, surface beacons should come before additional relays, because beacons supply the widely spaced lines of sight that relays in the same part of the sky cannot.
\end{remark}

\subsection{GNSS at Lunar Distance}
\label{sec:gnss}
The Lunar GNSS Receiver Experiment (LuGRE) on Blue Ghost Mission~1 obtained the first GNSS navigation fix on the lunar surface in March 2025, tracking GPS and Galileo signals at approximately \SI{356000}{km} from Earth~\cite{parker_lugre,nasa_lugre}. Because Earth GNSS is visible only from the near side, far-side users must obtain time and position from the lunar relay constellation. NASA has delivered NavCube3-mini, a GPS and Galileo receiver, to demonstrate GNSS-based navigation of a relay in lunar orbit~\cite{nasa_navcube3_2026}. The receiver will fly on the Altus-1 commercial relay, targeted for launch in 2027~\cite{mina2026altus}.

\subsection{Lunar Time Scale and the Navigation Signal}
\label{sec:timescale}
The relativistic rate offset between a clock on the lunar surface and Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) is \SI{56.02}{\micro\second\per day}~\cite{ashby_lunar_time}. The memorandum of the U.S. Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP) quotes \SI{58.7}{\micro\second\per day}~\cite{ostp_ltc}. That value holds for a clock orbiting Earth at the distance of the Moon, with the Moon's own gravitational potential neglected; adding that potential at the lunar surface gives the lower figure~\cite{ashby_lunar_time}. Kopeikin and Kaplan confirm the value independently and show that the periodic terms between lunar and terrestrial clocks stay below \SI{1}{\micro\second}~\cite{kopeikin_kaplan}. Over a lunar night, the offset accumulates to about \SI{0.83}{ms}, approximately \SI{250}{km} of range. The offset is predictable and can be removed by modeling, but only against a defined lunar time scale.

Three bodies are defining that scale, and LunaNet adopts it at service level. A United States policy directive tasks NASA with establishing Coordinated Lunar Time~\cite{ostp_ltc}. The General Assembly of the International Astronomical Union has adopted Resolutions~II and~III, which establish the Lunar Celestial Reference System and Lunar Coordinate Time (TCL) and invite international agreement on a lunar time standard~\cite{iau_2024_resolutions}. The Consultative Committee for Time and Frequency of the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) confirmed, in Recommendation CCTF-24-1 (2024), a task group to define and realize a lunar time scale traceable to UTC. The task group also prepares a draft resolution for the 28th General Conference on Weights and Measures~\cite{bipm_cctf24_1}. On the service side, LunaNet refers its services to a LunaNet Reference Time, and a provider whose own system time is offset from it must estimate and disseminate that offset~\cite{lnis_v5}.

Two realizations are under discussion. The first is the coordinate time TCL itself, which Bourgoin \emph{et al.} recommend~\cite{bourgoin_ltc}. The second is a scaled time whose rate matches clocks on a lunar equipotential surface~\cite{ashby_lunar_time}; it underlies the LunaNet statement that Coordinated Lunar Time will be referenced to the lunar geoid~\cite{dafesh_afs}. Turyshev gives closed-form mappings among these time scales, with two-way time-transfer corrections, at sub-picosecond accuracy~\cite{turyshev_2026}. In the Intuitive Machines LCRNS design, a GNSS receiver steers each relay's onboard time to GPS time, an ultra-stable oscillator holds it between updates, and crosslinks and surface receivers coordinate the lunar time system~\cite{ansalone_lcrns_time}.

The relays broadcast time and position in the Augmented Forward Signal (AFS) of the LunaNet signal-in-space standard~\cite{lsis_afs}. The signal is centered at \SI{2492.028}{MHz}, within the S-band lunar navigation allocation of \SI{2483.5}{}--\SI{2500}{MHz} of SFCG Recommendation~32-2R7~\cite{sfcg_rec32}. An in-phase data channel (BPSK(1), \SI{1.023}{Mchip/s}, 500~symbols/s, coded with a low-density parity-check (LDPC) code) and a dataless quadrature pilot (BPSK(5), \SI{5.115}{Mchip/s}) share the power equally. The pilot codes follow the construction of the GPS L1C Weil codes~\cite{lsis_afs,gramling_lunanet_pnt}. The AFS supports one-way ranging, in which the user only receives. The receiver measures the pseudorange $\rho_i = \lVert \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_u \rVert + c(\delta t_u - \delta t_i) + \varepsilon_i$ to relay~$i$. The relay broadcasts its clock offset $\delta t_i$, and the receiver solves for its own clock offset $\delta t_u$ together with position. A user of unknown position needs four relays, a surface node at a surveyed site needs one to transfer time, and any number of users can be served because users only receive. Two-way ranging measures the round trip on the user's own clock, so $\delta t_u$ cancels. Only the frequency error accumulated over the round trip remains, $\tfrac{1}{2} c\, y\, T_{\mathrm{rt}}$, which is \SI{2}{cm} for the \SI{0.12}{s} round trip to an ELFO relay and a quartz oscillator with $y = 10^{-9}$. Two-way ranging needs a transmitter at the user and relay resources for each user. LunaNet provides two-way measurements as a separate service of the provider's node~\cite{lnis_v5}.

A one-way user must hold its clock between contacts. Table~\ref{tab:holdover} gives the free-running time error of a temperature-compensated (TCXO) and an oven-controlled crystal oscillator (OCXO), a chip-scale atomic clock (CSAC), an ultra-stable quartz oscillator (USO) and the Deep Space Atomic Clock (DSAC). The error follows from $x(\tau)\approx \tau\sigma_y + \tfrac12 D\tau^2$, with time and frequency set at the last contact, $\sigma_y$ at its specified value, the drift $D$ uncorrected and temperature effects excluded. The USO drift is the value measured in flight on New Horizons shortly after launch, and \SI{1}{ns} is about \SI{0.3}{m} of range. The oscillator data come from~\cite{vig_tutorial} (TCXO and OCXO), \cite{microchip_csac} (CSAC), \cite{enzer2013,weaver2016} (USO) and~\cite{burt2021} (DSAC). Over a \SI{1}{h} gap the crystal and chip-scale clocks accumulate errors of \SI{3.2}{}--\SI{38}{ns}, roughly \SI{1}{}--\SI{11}{m} of range, whereas the TCXO reaches \SI{3.7}{\micro\second}. Over a 14-day gap, half a lunar day, uncorrected frequency aging dominates, and the error grows to hundreds of microseconds. The error falls to the \SI{0.1}{}--\SI{12}{\micro\second} of the stochastic term only if the aging rate has been estimated over earlier contacts. Of the oscillators in Table~\ref{tab:holdover}, only an atomic standard of the DSAC class, at $3\times10^{-15}$ after 23 days~\cite{burt2021}, holds nanosecond-level time over a lunar night. The reference constellation keeps at least one relay in geometric view of the south pole at all times (Fig.~\ref{fig:contacts}), so terrain shadowing and scheduling gaps set the holdover interval. A surface node that loses the relay for days therefore needs either an atomic clock or two-way time transfer when contact resumes.

\section{Protocols Across Intermittency}
\label{sec:protocol}
Section~\ref{sec:payload} established that the relay must host a bundle node, which stores data units (bundles) and forwards them when a link becomes available. This section specifies the protocols that node runs. Intermittent connectivity and the Earth--Moon light-time delay jointly govern how protocols must be designed for the lunar environment. The one-way Earth--Moon propagation delay is \SI{1.28}{\second} and the round trip is \SI{2.56}{\second}. No request-response transaction can complete faster than this round trip, whatever the modulation, coding, or protocol. Latency can be reduced only by relocating the responding function closer to the user, whether through on-board processing, edge caching or local authentication. Fig.~\ref{fig:stack} shows the stacks this section connects. The surface runs 3GPP, whereas the relay and the Earth station run CCSDS space-link protocols with delay-tolerant networking above them. A gateway, specified by no standard yet, converts 3GPP bearers into bundles when the relay sets (drops below the local horizon). The section first states what 3GPP non-terrestrial networks provide and lack, then maps the three standards families onto each other. It then defines the boundary between the 3GPP surface and the delay-tolerant relay, states when a traffic class can be served in real time, and shows how contact plans drive routing.

TCP works only up to a certain delay, because its timers expect fast acknowledgments. Wood \emph{et al.} characterize this delay range through two radii fixed by those timers: a performance radius and a larger protocol radius, beyond which TCP cannot complete its exchanges. With the \SI{3}{\second} initial retransmission timeout then in force, the first performance radius corresponds to \SI{1.5}{\second} of one-way delay and the limiting protocol radius, set by SYN retries, to \SI{22.5}{\second}. The authors place direct Earth--Moon TCP inside the first radius, although with poor use of link capacity~\cite{wood_tcp_radius}. RFC~6298 has since lowered the initial timeout to \SI{1}{\second}~\cite{rfc6298}, which moves the first performance radius to \SI{0.5}{\second} of one-way delay and places the Earth--Moon round trip beyond it. Table~\ref{tab:timers} lists the principal timer failures.

Delay-tolerant protocols are designed for this regime. The Bundle Protocol version~7 (BPv7) provides store-carry-forward delivery, in which a node holds data until the next hop becomes reachable. BPv7, together with its TCP convergence-layer protocol version~4~\cite{rfc9174}, is on the IETF Standards Track~\cite{rfc9171}. Custody transfer, in which a node takes responsibility for delivering a bundle onward, was part of the earlier version of the protocol. It has been moved out of BPv7 into a separate bundle-in-bundle encapsulation specification~\cite{rfc9171}. LunaNet lists it as an extension still being defined~\cite{lnis_v5}. The Licklider Transmission Protocol provides reliable delivery over long-delay links~\cite{rfc5326}. Contact Graph Routing (CGR) computes routes over scheduled contacts, the planned intervals during which two nodes can communicate~\cite{burleigh_cgr}. The KPLO payload exercised custody transfer and retransmission under induced outage in lunar orbit (Section~\ref{sec:assets})~\cite{kim_kplo_dtn}. A testbed comparison for the Earth--Moon scenario found that QUIC outperforms TCP end to end, and that DTN with QUIC or LTP convergence layers can outperform both~\cite{e2e_vs_dtn_2026}.

\subsection{What 3GPP Non-Terrestrial Networks Provide, and What They Lack}
Release~19 adds two features relevant to the lunar regime: regenerative payloads, which carry the 5G base station (gNB) on board instead of only relaying the signal; and store-and-forward operation with an on-board LTE base station (eNB) for Internet-of-Things NTN (IoT-NTN)~\cite{3gpp_rel19_ntn}. Release~20, in progress in 2026, addresses operation that tolerates a temporary loss of GNSS~\cite{3gpp_rp261568}. These additions partially address the lunar delay regime, but no 3GPP work item explicitly targets the lunar or cislunar environment; the scope remains Earth-orbit constellations.

The links to Earth exceed the \SI{270.73}{ms} GEO delay of the 3GPP non-terrestrial specifications~\cite{3gpp_tr_38821}, and with it the design range of the 3GPP timers, whereas the relay links fall within it (dashed line in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(b)). The specifications also assume continuous connectivity and a GNSS fix at the terminal, and they limit store-and-forward to IoT-NTN. A lunar network, in contrast, has contact gaps of hours, has no Earth GNSS on the far side, and needs store-and-forward for broadband and bulk data (Table~\ref{tab:rosetta_radio}). On the far side, timing advance (how early a terminal must transmit for its signal to arrive in its slot) must therefore be obtained by two-way ranging with the relay or from the lunar navigation service (Section~\ref{sec:timescale}).

\subsection{CCSDS, 3GPP and IETF DTN Standards}
A lunar network is specified by three standards families at once. The space-link and file-transfer layers follow CCSDS, which LunaNet adopts as its interoperability baseline~\cite{lnis_v5,ccsds_lunar_standards}. The surface access layer, where it is proposed, follows 3GPP. The store-and-forward layer follows the IETF delay-tolerant networking suite, which CCSDS has itself adopted as a companion series~\cite{ccsds_bp,ccsds_ltp}. The three describe overlapping functions in incompatible terms, and an engineer reading across them encounters the same function under three names. Table~\ref{tab:rosetta} maps the functions for which the three families use different mechanisms, omitting those that differ only in name. The mechanisms differ mainly in what they cover: a CCSDS mechanism is scoped to a single link, a 3GPP mechanism to an attached subscriber, and a DTN mechanism to a data unit that persists beyond both the link and the attachment.

\subsection{Interworking Between 3GPP and DTN}
A lunar surface network built on 3GPP NR provides real-time cellular service within a relay's footprint. When the relay sets, an end-to-end 3GPP session to Earth fails, because its timers, handover procedures and authentication exchanges assume continuous connectivity. Only a core network hosted on the surface, which handles authentication and sessions locally, keeps local service running. Neither protocol stack natively handles the transition from 3GPP operation to delay-tolerant operation. The Release~19 store-and-forward mode serves only IoT-NTN, and BPv7 does not define how a 3GPP bearer should be encapsulated into a bundle.

The boundary therefore requires a gateway function, and two designs are possible. The first is a \emph{protocol proxy} on the surface gateway that terminates the 3GPP stack, buffers pending traffic, and injects it into the DTN as bundles when the relay reappears. This design preserves the 3GPP user-plane interface for the rover but requires the gateway to maintain 3GPP state across the outage. The second is a \emph{convergence layer} that maps 3GPP bearers directly onto BPv7 bundles without proxying, treating the DTN as a transport substrate for 3GPP traffic. This design avoids holding 3GPP state at the gateway but requires modifications to the 3GPP stack. Neither 3GPP nor the IETF has specified such a mapping. Either design can be prototyped on the network emulation testbeds for space DTN surveyed by Bull \emph{et al.}~\cite{perreault_testbed}.

The GEM emulation testbed at NASA Glenn addresses the 3GPP side of the boundary. It runs srsRAN 4G base-station and user-equipment software on software-defined radios through a hardware channel emulator loaded with ray-traced lunar channel models. The testbed has emulated two notional EVA traverses of Artemis~III, in its earlier crewed-landing plan, served by an eNB and packet core hosted on the lander. These emulations reproduce the expected drops in SNR and throughput during terrain shadowing~\cite{nasa_gem_2026}. The testbed, as reported in 2026, does not include a DTN segment, and the latency cost of the 3GPP-to-DTN transition has not been reported.

\subsection{Real Time or Store-and-Forward}
Whether a traffic class can be served across the contact gaps is decided by the two comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:admissibility}: its bundle lifetime against the largest gap, and its rate against the effective backhaul, the backhaul rate averaged over contacts and gaps. The published requirements fix the real-time side only partially. An ESA-supported study of quasi-real-time traffic over the Earth--Moon DTN link evaluates bundle delay against a \SI{2.5}{\second} threshold~\cite{ulierte_traffic_prio}. The NASA relay services requirements document allots each relay node less than \SI{1}{\second} (TBR) of processing latency for real-time data, excluding light time, within an estimated end-to-end budget of \SI{5}{\second}~\cite{nasa_lunar_relay_srd}. LunaNet defines the two service types without a numerical boundary between them (Section~\ref{sec:assets}).

\subsection{Scheduling and Contact Plans}
Contact Graph Routing~\cite{burleigh_cgr} computes the path through a delay-tolerant network by enumerating the contact opportunities as a directed graph. A contact opportunity is an interval during which two nodes have mutual visibility and sufficient link margin. The contact plan, which lists all contacts with their start time, duration, and data rate, is the input to CGR and must be generated before routing decisions can be made.

On the Moon the contact plan is deterministic, because relay orbits can be propagated to high precision and the margins of the lunar links are set by geometry. Only the Earth end of the trunk is subject to weather and station scheduling. The plan nevertheless varies in time. A solar-powered node that enters lunar night has no contacts. An ELFO relay dwells over the south pole near apolune and is out of view for several hours around each perilune. A rover behind a ridge is out of contact until it moves into view. The plan therefore varies with the power state of each node, the orbit of each relay and the terrain around each surface node. When resources do not allow every feasible contact to enter the plan, Fraire and Finochietto~\cite{fraire_contact_plan} select contacts by a multi-objective design that assigns links fairly while minimizing the all-to-all route delay under CGR. Because the exact model becomes intractable for large topologies, they propose heuristics.

The contact plan must be recomputed whenever the network topology changes. On the Moon, the topology changes on three timescales: orbital (hours, as relays enter and exit visibility), diurnal (weeks, as nodes enter and exit lunar night), and mission (months, as new assets are deployed). The LCRNS requirements accordingly call for onboard scheduling and autonomous initiation of stored-data transmission, with routing configurations updated according to the planned contacts~\cite{nasa_onboard_lunanet}. Onboard scheduling of this kind requires the regenerative payload discussed in Section~\ref{sec:payload}.





\section{Security, Power and Spectrum in Operation}
\label{sec:cross}
Three constraints outside the link budget also shape a lunar network. The first is security when no certificate authority can be reached. The second is power availability, which determines which nodes are active at a given time. The third is the regulation of the spectrum chosen in Section~\ref{sec:frequency}. The far side, where the three combine, is treated in Section~\ref{sec:farside}.

\subsection{Security Under Intermittent Connectivity}
Terrestrial public-key infrastructure (PKI), the system of certificate authorities that vouch for the keys of each node, relies on near-instant credential validation. The Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP) asks the authority whether a certificate has been revoked, and fresh OCSP responses are practical only for assets whose round-trip latency is of the order of milliseconds~\cite{foerster_pki}. A lunar node is at a one-way light time of approximately \SI{1.28}{\second} from an Earth-based authority and may be out of contact with it for hours. OCSP requires direct contact with the issuing certificate authority and is therefore unsuitable in its intended form~\cite{koisser_pki_space}. The alternative is to fetch revocation information in advance, as cached certificate revocation lists, OCSP stapling or short-lived certificates. Prefetched information leaves a window in which a compromised credential retains its authority~\cite{foerster_pki}. On a lunar link that window is at least as long as the contact gap. Public-key infrastructure for space networks must tolerate certificate authorities subject to delay and disruption~\cite{foerster_pki,koisser_pki_space}, and location-based authentication has been proposed as a complementary mechanism for cislunar missions~\cite{jedermann_location_auth}.

Bundle Protocol Security (BPSec)~\cite{rfc9172} defines integrity and confidentiality services at the bundle layer. The specification leaves key management, and with it certificate revocation, to a separate network-management function. Replay protection is delegated to the security context, to bundle-lifetime checks and to application-level uniqueness mechanisms~\cite{rfc9172}. Access control and non-repudiation lie outside the services BPSec defines. A formal cryptographic analysis by Dowling \emph{et al.}~\cite{dowling_bpsec} establishes security under the default security context, identifies a weakness in message-loss detection, and proposes a strengthened construction. The BPSec COSE security context, an IETF Internet-Draft~\cite{bpsec_cose}, defines a COSE profile focused on asymmetric-key algorithms and a PKIX certificate profile for BPSec interoperation. It evaluates certificate validity against the bundle creation time and does not expect verifiers to have the continuous connectivity that OCSP requires. The draft explicitly excludes the issuance and provisioning of certificates and the distribution of revocation lists, so the delay-tolerant revocation problem identified in~\cite{foerster_pki,koisser_pki_space} remains open.

Several works address key management and revocation. Koisser \emph{et al.}~\cite{foerster_pki} propose a PKI architecture for space networks whose credential-validation layer tolerates delay and disruption. Nodes pass compact, verifiable revocation data to each other peer to peer, so updates spread epidemically through the network. In constellation-scale simulation, critical revocation updates propagate orders of magnitude faster than with certificate revocation lists or OCSP stapling. A systematization-of-knowledge study by the same group~\cite{koisser_pki_space} surveys PKI and location-privacy challenges in space networks more broadly. It identifies the predictable topology of orbital networks as a property that can be exploited to disseminate revocation information efficiently. The study treats location privacy chiefly as the risk that satellites intercepting ground-user transmissions can triangulate the position of the user. Montilla and Montilla~Ochoa~\cite{montilla_dtn_security} present LunaNet-specific security considerations for DTN service providers. They note that BPSec deployment requires tight coordination and administrative trust among the parties involved. Shared security contexts and key distribution are among the policies on which interconnected LunaNet service providers must agree.

Three open problems remain. First, to our knowledge, no deployed system performs certificate revocation over a link whose staleness is measured in hours; the epidemic revocation scheme of~\cite{foerster_pki} has been evaluated only in simulation. Second, replay protection rejects old messages that an attacker resends, and across long outages it requires time-window validation against a shared time reference. Lunar time itself is still being defined (Section~\ref{sec:timescale}), so the security and timing architectures depend on each other. Third, the LunaNet interoperability specification~\cite{lnis_v5} references BPSec but mandates neither BPSec nor any security context. Its security section is informational and defers requirements to a separate LunaNet security specification, which version~5 lists as to be determined. Interoperability at the security layer therefore remains unresolved. A concrete protocol combining bundle-layer integrity, cached revocation, and time-window replay protection, together with a formal security proof grounded in the lunar timing reference, remains to be developed.



\subsection{Power as a Network Variable}
\label{sec:power}
Terrestrial network design usually treats power as supplied by the grid. On the Moon, power affects the design in three ways. The power bus of the platform caps the transmit power (Section~\ref{sec:frequency}). Power availability determines whether a surface node is active, and it is also a criterion for selecting sites. Table~\ref{tab:power} summarizes the sources and Fig.~\ref{fig:powertimeline} the power state of each node over one lunar day, which sets the timeline for the contact plan of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. In the figure, only the radioisotope and fission nodes stay on through the night. The ridge mast remains powered except during periods of darkness of up to a few days, and the relay loses power only in short eclipses. The solar nodes shut down or enter a survival mode, so the contact plan changes at each sunset and sunrise.

A solar-powered surface node must operate through the \SI{354}{h} lunar night, and the energy storage required for that night dominates the mass of a photovoltaic power system~\cite{colozza_power1991}. For a \SI{20}{W} night load the stored energy is \SI{7.08}{kWh}, which at a battery specific energy of \SI{100}{Wh/kg} and full depth of discharge corresponds to approximately \SI{71}{kg} of cells. At lunar-base scale, hybrid solar-nuclear systems minimize mass in a parametric study of power configurations~\cite{balat_power2021}. For comparison, the LuSEE-Night instrument carries a \SI{7.16}{kWh} lithium-ion battery of \SI{50}{kg}, within a total instrument mass limit of \SI{128}{kg}, to operate through the \SI{328}{h} night at its landing site~\cite{aguirre_lusee}.

Because the power state of each node determines whether it is active, the network topology varies over the lunar day. Contact Graph Routing accommodates scheduled contacts~\cite{burleigh_cgr}. The contact plan itself, however, must be derived from the illumination of each node, and hence from its position and the date. This dependence couples the routing problem to the power system. Battery-aware contact plan design, developed for a small-satellite constellation in low Earth orbit, incorporates a model of solar infeed and on-board battery state into contact plan construction to control the risk of battery depletion~\cite{fraire_battery_cp}.

\subsection{The Far Side as a Distinct Regime}
\label{sec:farside}
Three properties established earlier combine on the far side and make it a separate design case. First, no direct-to-Earth link exists at any aperture or power, so all traffic passes through a relay (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}). Second, no Earth navigation signal arrives, so position and time come from the lunar service or from ranging with the relay (Section~\ref{sec:timescale}). Third, most of the far side lies within the Shielded Zone, the region protected for radio astronomy, where users such as the LuSEE-Night low-frequency instrument are the protected party (Section~\ref{sec:spectrum}). Because the Moon blocks terrestrial emissions there, the far side is also a radio environment sought by low-frequency astronomy~\cite{itu_ra479}, and the observatories planned there are expected to be both the main users of a far-side network and a constraint on its emissions. A longitude-averaged coverage statistic does not capture these differences; the design cases are the near side, the poles with Earth at low elevation, and the far side.

\subsection{Spectrum Regulation and the Shielded Zone}
\label{sec:spectrum}
The band plan of the contracted lunar infrastructure (S-band for surface access and navigation, Ka-band for trunks and backhaul) has a different origin from terrestrial band plans (Table~\ref{tab:governance}). The treaty-level authority is the same. The Radio Regulations of the ITU allocate frequency bands to radiocommunication services irrespective of location. A station on the lunar surface or in lunar orbit is a space station in the sense of the Regulations. Lunar links therefore fall under the space research and space operation service allocations, with surface-to-surface and orbit-to-surface links classed as space-to-space. No identification for IMT, the regulatory basis of terrestrial 3GPP bands, exists for the Moon, which is why the only cellular network flown to date operated under a mission-specific waiver~\cite{mit_tr_nokia}. Below the treaty level, the de facto lunar band plan is written by the SFCG, whose Recommendation 32-2 is non-binding and is enforced through procurement, by being written into the LunaNet interoperability specification and into NASA's relay contracts. Licensing is national, as on Earth. The filing templates, however, describe Earth orbits, and lunar orbits are notified by established practice because no rule covers them. Coordination between lunar networks of different administrations relies on the SFCG and on bilateral agreements, which confer no priority right, whereas Earth orbit has formal procedures and first-come-first-served coordination status.

No.~22.22 of the Radio Regulations prohibits emissions causing harmful interference to radio astronomy and other passive services in the entire frequency spectrum. These thresholds are intended to quantify the protection criteria that far-side emitters must meet.

\section{Status of Lunar Propagation Measurements}
\label{sec:measurement}
Every surface result in Sections~\ref{sec:budgets} to~\ref{sec:space} depends on a terrain propagation model, which predicts the signal loss between two points over lunar terrain. The relay-link results, in contrast, assume free-space propagation. This section states which models exist, which data could validate them, and what is missing. Recommendation ITU-R P.2170-0 defines the Irregular Lunar Model for point-to-area and point-to-point attenuation over lunar terrain. The model treats the exosphere as free space and accounts for terrain irregularity and regolith permittivity between \SI{20}{MHz} and \SI{37}{GHz}~\cite{itu_p2170}. NTIA/ITS maintains a public example implementation~\cite{ntia_ilm_github}. To our knowledge, the Irregular Lunar Model is the only standardized lunar surface propagation model. A preliminary draft revision is under development in ITU-R Working Party~3J~\cite{itu_p2170_revision}.

Several non-standard models complement it. Wang and Wyglinski~\cite{wang_ojcoms2025} propose a ring-based channel model for lunar 5G links that accounts for obstruction, multipath and regolith properties. They compare it with measurements from the NASA Desert Research and Technology Studies (DRATS), field tests at a lunar-analog site on Earth. Their predictions match these measurements more closely than the terrestrial Irregular Terrain Model and its ITWOM variant. JPL studies collected by the Lunar Surface Propagation project of NASA Glenn Research Center~\cite{nasa_grc_lsp_2026} model south-polar fading to the DSN~\cite{margot_southpole_fading}, LRO-to-DSN multipath~\cite{sanchez_net_multipath_2020} and coherent reflection on direct-to-Earth links, validated against Chandrayaan-3 and IM-1 data~\cite{sanchez_net_coherent_2025}. A site-specific path-loss model at \SI{5.855}{GHz} for Shoemaker Rim~F at the lunar south pole is derived from 3D ray tracing over a high-resolution digital elevation map. The path-loss exponent sets how fast loss grows with distance (2 in free space). The model reports exponents of 2.54 for dipole-dipole and 4.33 for omni-dipole configurations~\cite{shoemaker_rim_2026}. Link-budget planning must therefore account for this strong sensitivity to the transmitter antenna pattern.

The Irregular Lunar Model has not been validated against in-situ propagation data. It adapts the Longley--Rice irregular-terrain formulation to lunar geometry and uses LOLA elevation data for terrain and permittivity relations derived from Apollo samples for the regolith~\cite{itu_p2170,ntia_ilm_github,olhoeft_strangway1975}; Chang'e-5 measurements at 0.2--12.2~GHz have since revised those relations~\cite{dong_dielectric_ce5}. The only calibrated surface-to-surface measurements, from the Apollo~17 Surface Electrical Properties experiment, were made at 1--32~MHz over ranges up to \SI{1.6}{km}~\cite{grimm_sep2018}. The available empirical data comprise the LRO Ka-band signal-level record (Section~\ref{sec:trunk})~\cite{lro_ka_analysis}, the direct-to-Earth and LRO-to-DSN multipath observations cited above, the ROLSES-1 surface recordings of terrestrial shortwave transmissions~\cite{hibbard_rolses2025}, the LLCD and LOCL optical results~\cite{boroson_llcd,giggenbach_locl}, the DRATS terrestrial analog campaign~\cite{nasa_drats2022}, the KPLO delay-tolerant networking test~\cite{kim_kplo_dtn} and the Chang'e-5 dielectric measurements~\cite{dong_dielectric_ce5}. No calibrated measurements exist of surface-to-surface propagation at VHF or higher frequencies, or of surface-to-relay fading at low elevation over lunar terrain. The channel within permanently shadowed regions and the surface interference environment from Earth-based emitters above the HF band are also unmeasured, and so are the Doppler and range-rate statistics of the service link seen from a polar site.

A first campaign adequate to close this gap would require at least two surface nodes, one fixed and one mobile, logging calibrated signal strength at S-band and Ka-band across mare, highland, crater-rim and shadowed-region terrain over a complete day-night cycle. Such a data set would either validate or refute the Irregular Lunar Model and would supply the basis for improved channel models~\cite{itu_p2170,zhu_channel_ray}. A contracted source of such data is the NASA 5G contract of September 2026, which covers radio propagation measurements and a flight demonstration of a 5G and Wi-Fi surface network that runs to the end of 2028~\cite{nasa_c26014}. NASA's 2026 architecture-driven data gaps list the same need. Gap DN-034 asks for measurements of how the lunar surface reflects and absorbs communication signals aimed near the horizon, including shadowing and interference between surface elements, and gap DN-002 asks for south-polar elevation maps at better than \SI{1}{m} per pixel, partly to site line-of-sight systems such as antennas~\cite{nasa_datagaps2026}.


Before such a campaign, hardware channel emulation provides an interim substitute: a laboratory emulator applies a modeled channel to live radio signals. In~\cite{baena2027surface}, the ray-traced tiles are exported as tapped-delay channels within the tap and quantization limits of two hardware channel emulators. A full 5G stack can then run over Connecting Ridge and Shackleton channels on software-defined radios. Emulation allows the results of a future measurement campaign to be compared with protocol-level predictions as well as path-loss predictions.


\section{An Architecture for the Sustained Moon}
\label{sec:architecture}
Section~\ref{sec:measurement} showed that missing measurements limit the accuracy of the quantitative results. Those results still inform design choices, and this section examines them. The preceding sections take the contracted infrastructure as given. This section asks whether that infrastructure suits the sustained-presence Moon of Table~\ref{tab:phases}, with bases, commercial operators and far-side observatories. It then asks what form a network specified for that Moon would take. 

\subsection{Origins of the Current Infrastructure Design}
The relay and navigation systems under contract were specified between 2022 and 2024 for robotic science and for crewed sorties of a week: few users, one polar cap, scheduled contacts, and every bit terminating on Earth. Their specifications reflect these assumptions: five satellites, \SI{1}{}--\SI{50}{Mbps} per user, one cap, and a requirements document that allots each relay a processing allowance but no routing among relays~\cite{nasa_lunar_relay_srd}. This design applies the deep-space network model to the Moon: routing is centered on Earth, and each agency operates its own segment. This model matches the demand of Table~\ref{tab:phases} for the sortie phase only. Meanwhile, stated program objectives have shifted from science missions to sustained presence and resource extraction. Two groups of agencies are building parallel infrastructures: the Artemis partners around LunaNet, and the International Lunar Research Station partners around the Chinese relays. Each group has its own band usage, its own time reference and its own interoperability baseline. Both intend to operate on the far side, where uncoordinated networks could interfere with each other and with the radio astronomy protected in the Shielded Zone (Section~\ref{sec:spectrum}). Because the first-generation lunar design decisions are being made in the current procurement cycle, this section evaluates them as well as explaining their origin.

\subsection{Limits of Direct-to-Satellite Surface Access}
This subsection examines whether surface users can rely on relays alone, without surface infrastructure. The link budgets show that a handset-class omnidirectional S-band terminal falls about \SI{55}{dB} short of filling a \SI{20}{MHz} channel to an ELFO relay at \SI{15000}{}--\SI{17400}{km} (link~2, Section~\ref{sec:sixlinks}). Lowering the relay to \SI{150}{km} shortens the path by a factor of 100 to 116 and recovers about \SI{40}{dB} of this deficit, which leaves about \SI{15}{dB}. A relay antenna with \SI{15}{dB} more gain than the \SI{0.5}{m} dish assumed, an array of about \SI{3}{m}, would close it. Direct-to-cell satellites in Earth orbit, which serve unmodified phones from \SI{550}{km}, use large phased arrays for the same purpose. The obstacles are satellite count and cost. Low lunar orbits are perturbed by the mass concentrations below the lunar surface and need stationkeeping except at the frozen inclinations (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}). Each pass lasts minutes, so continuous coverage of one site would require several tens of satellites. Delivery is also costly. NASA estimated about \$1~million per kilogram delivered to the surface under its commercial lander services~\cite{oig2024clps}. Published commercial prices are \$0.3~million per kilogram to lunar orbit and \$1.2 to 1.5~million to the surface~\cite{astrobotic2021pug,ispace2024brief}, against about \$2700 per kilogram to LEO~\cite{jones2018launch}. At these prices a \SI{100}{kg} low-orbit relay costs about \$30~million to deliver, so delivering a low-orbit tier of several dozen satellites would cost on the order of \$1~billion. For comparison, ispace has contracted \SI{500}{kg} of Starship capacity to the Moon for \$50~million, for launch no earlier than 2030~\cite{ispace2026starship}. Such a constellation would become cost-effective if the user population grows from about ten users to about a thousand sensors and robots, or when heavy-lift landing lowers the cost per kilogram. The preferred architecture therefore depends on the user count, which is expected to grow in the sustained-presence phase.

\subsection{A Proposed Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} sketches a network specified for the sustained-presence Moon of Table~\ref{tab:phases}. Each element is motivated by a result earlier in the paper.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A two-tier constellation.} Tier~1 consists of the current systems: high-dwell relays in eccentric or L2 orbits, which stay above the pole for hours, for coverage, trunking to Earth and navigation. Tier~2 adds a low-orbit tier for direct-to-device access and capacity. The pair is analogous to a combined GEO and LEO architecture on Earth. Tier~1 is sized by dwell and by the aperture rule (Section~\ref{sec:gain}); tier~2 is sized by user count.
\item \emph{Mountain-top repeaters.} The Moon has no synchronous orbit (Section~\ref{sec:orbitfam}), but a \SI{2}{km} polar massif has a radio horizon of \SI{83}{km} against \SI{6}{km} for a \SI{10}{m} mast (Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon}). A small number of repeaters on the most illuminated ridges would therefore serve the terrain in line of sight within that horizon with local-cell latency. The sunlight that makes those ridges candidate base sites would also power the repeaters. Such repeaters give that terrain continuous visibility, as a synchronous orbit would, and they move the fine-teleoperation and telepresence classes of Table~\ref{tab:traffic} out of the relay's duty cycle.
\item \emph{A millimeter-wave surface backbone.} Millimeter-wave radio links can connect the fixed assets: habitat, power plant, landing pad and instruments. Between such assets, the ladder of Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} gives gigabits per second over \SI{10}{km} with decimeter apertures and watts in any band above Ka. These gigabit-per-second fixed links avoid laying cable on the regolith. Bandwidth is the limiting resource, and dust on the apertures is the open issue.
\item \emph{A Moon-centric core.} A local 5G core, local delay-tolerant routing, a lunar time reference and local key management. With these functions on the Moon, base and swarm traffic, which Table~\ref{tab:traffic} assigns to the surface cell or mesh, remains on the Moon and off the trunk link to Earth.
\item \emph{An inter-relay mesh.} Links among relays between E-band and about \SI{240}{GHz}, or optical, so that relays route among themselves and the Earth trunk carries only what is bound for Earth.
\item \emph{A shared far-side relay.} A far-side relay needs an orbit that sees the far side and Earth together, an L2 halo as for Queqiao or an ELFO as for Queqiao-2. Because both groups of agencies plan to operate there, a far-side relay operated as shared international infrastructure would avoid two uncoordinated networks in the Shielded Zone, with its emissions coordinated with the observatories it serves.
\item \emph{Spectrum above \SI{28.35}{GHz} ahead of deployment.} The regulatory work of Section~\ref{sec:spectrum} should be completed before the first relay operating above \SI{28.35}{GHz} is launched.
\end{itemize}

A Moon-centric core requires deciding where computation is placed: at the instrument, at the gateway or on the relay. The limited trunk capacity sets how much processing each location performs. At the instrument, LuSEE-Night is designed to form correlation spectra in a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) before relaying them~\cite{bale_lusee_2023}. The FARSIDE array concept correlates 128 antennas at its base station into \SI{65}{GB} per day, with \SI{192}{GB} of storage for three days of outage~\cite{burns_farside_2019}. At the gateway, the local core and the 5G-to-DTN boundary of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} keep base traffic on the Moon. On the relay, the LCRNS requirements imply bundle and Internet Protocol processing at about \SI{100}{Mbps} per node~\cite{nasa_onboard_lunanet}. Lunar data-storage payloads have flown only as demonstrations: a storage payload on IM-1 exchanged files in transit, in lunar orbit and on the surface~\cite{dcd_lonestar_im1}, and a successor flew on IM-2~\cite{storagenl_freedom_2025,nasa_im2}.

\subsection{In-Situ Propagation Validation}
The Irregular Lunar Model of Recommendation ITU-R P.2170-0 predicts propagation loss over lunar terrain, but it has not been validated against in-situ data~\cite{itu_p2170}. No announced mission, including the 3GPP demonstration studied for the first crewed landings~\cite{nasa_gem_2026}, planned from Artemis~IV~\cite{nasa_art3_2026}, includes a calibrated propagation campaign across several terrain types. Closing the gap requires the campaign set out in Section~\ref{sec:measurement}.

\subsection{Proactive Handover in Binary Coverage}
Horizon maps derived from elevation models already predict terrain occlusion of relay passes for the Curiosity rover~\cite{wiederhold_mars_occlusion} and have been applied to lunar lander--rover planning~\cite{menon_teamindus}, but the error of such a predictor, set by the elevation-model resolution and the rover position uncertainty, has not been characterized. The deliverable is a visibility predictor that combines the elevation model, the relay ephemeris and the rover position, runs within the time to occlusion, and states the handover overlap needed to compensate for its residual error.

\subsection{Passive Reflectors for Shadow-Zone Coverage}
A passive reflector is a flat or shaped panel at radio frequencies, or a mirror for optical links, placed at an obstructing rim. It would add a second path into a shadowed region without consuming power or adding a node to the routing layer. A corner-cube retroreflector is unsuitable, because it returns the wave toward its source. Apart from a patent disclosure~\cite{sercel_patent_shadowed}, no quantitative link analysis of passive reflectors for lunar surface coverage has been published. What is needed is a budget of the reflected leg that includes path loss, alignment tolerance and the fraction of the relay ephemeris during which the reflected path exists.

\subsection{Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces for Shadow-Zone Coverage}
Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces are panels whose elements set their reflection electronically, so that they redirect an incident wave. They have been proposed for cislunar links~\cite{ris_cislunar2024} and qualitatively for a crater edge on the Moon~\cite{liyanaarachchi_cislunar}, and analyzed for crater configurations on Mars~\cite{koktas_ris_mars}. The lunar case of a fixed surface that redirects a relay signal behind a ridge to a rover in shadow has not been analyzed quantitatively. That analysis should compare the cost of emplacing the surface with that of a second surface relay.

\subsection{Joint Communications and Navigation Constellation Design}
Communications favors few satellites with long dwell and navigation many satellites over several planes (Fig.~\ref{fig:commnav}). Joint orbital designs that serve both functions have been reported~\cite{bhamidipati_constellation_2023}. Three surface ranging beacons have been shown to match the geometry of a twenty-four-satellite orbital constellation~\cite{baweja_dop}. However, no published design optimizes orbital satellites and surface beacons jointly for communications and navigation. Such an optimization should be evaluated on availability, data volume and dilution of precision.

\subsection{Timing Advance Without GNSS}
The 3GPP non-terrestrial specifications through Release~19 assume a GNSS fix at the terminal, and the Release~20 work addresses only its temporary loss~\cite{3gpp_rp261568}. The deliverable is a timing-advance procedure based on two-way ranging with the relay or on the lunar navigation service, with its signaling, attainable accuracy and interaction with Doppler pre-compensation specified.

\subsection{Positioning Reference Signals for Lunar Localization}
The 5G~NR positioning reference signal is the downlink signal from which a terminal measures its range to each transmitter. Doppler-induced inter-carrier and inter-symbol interference in this signal grows with the number of satellites in view~\cite{prs_ntn_plans2023}. At the south pole, the visible satellites cluster in a narrow cone~\cite{baweja_dop}. The configuration of the signal for this regime has not been studied. The deliverable is a reference-signal configuration, broadcast from relays or surface beacons, for the south pole and for the far side, where no Earth GNSS signal is available.

\subsection{Integrated Sensing and Communications}
Integrated sensing and communications has been studied for non-terrestrial networks~\cite{isac_ntn2026} and, at the Moon, for debris detection with the Ka-band relay of the Gateway design~\cite{dong_cislunarsense}; the waveform, the resolution attainable against surface terrain and the allocation of resources between sensing and communications have not been characterized for the lunar surface. The characterization is needed for a relay waveform that also detects terrain obstacles for a rover.

\section{Roadmap to 2035}
\label{sec:roadmap}
The open problems of Section~\ref{sec:open} differ in urgency, and this section orders them in time, together with the architecture of Section~\ref{sec:architecture}. Fig.~\ref{fig:roadmap} divides the next decade into three horizons by the state of the infrastructure. For each horizon, it lists the missions and contracted assets that define it, the standards milestones that fall within it, the design problem that dominates it and the open problems it makes actionable. The three horizons cover 2026--2028, 2028--2032 and the years from 2032. They are defined by capability, so the dates are indicative and may be delayed without changing the order of the horizons. The dates in the figure are agency and operator targets (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). For landers and relays, the next commercial landers are targeted from November 2026 and the Altus-1 and Lunar Pathfinder relays for 2027~\cite{brinkmann2026,mina2026altus,sstl_pathfinder_mission}. For crewed and base milestones, Artemis~III is an Earth-orbit test in 2027, crewed landings follow from Artemis~IV in 2028~\cite{nasa_art3_2026}. The Moon Base phases run to 2029, from 2029 to 2032 and from 2032~\cite{nasa_moonbase}, and Gateway was paused in March 2026~\cite{nasa_ignition2026}. For navigation, power and regulation, Moonlight initial and full operations are targeted by 2028 and 2030 according to ESA~\cite{esa_moonlight_overview} and in 2029 and 2031 according to its prime contractor~\cite{telespazio_moonlight_web}. The pilot of the Chinese constellation is expected around 2030~\cite{ilrs_relay_constellation}, and Lunar Reactor-1 is targeted for 2030~\cite{nasa_sr1_2026}. The World Radiocommunication Conference WRC-27 meets in October and November 2027~\cite{itu_wrc27}.

The first axis of the roadmap is technology. Four trends span the horizons, three in technology and one in power. The first two follow from the aperture rule of Section~\ref{sec:gain}. The first is the move to higher bands of the frequency ladder on links whose two ends both have fixed apertures. With no atmosphere between relays, or between a relay and a surface gateway, the carrier frequency is limited only by pointing, power and noise. The inter-satellite and backhaul links are therefore expected to migrate from Ka-band to the range from E-band to about \SI{240}{GHz}, in which the rate is near its maximum. This migration would follow once tubes of tens of watts, in laboratory development in 2026, qualify for flight. Where microradian pointing is feasible, they can move to optical links (Section~\ref{sec:frequency}). The regulatory work for these bands has not begun. The second is the replacement of mechanically steered reflectors by electronically steered arrays on relays and gateways. An electronically steered array decouples the beamwidth that closes the link from the attitude control of the platform, so one relay can serve several surface users and a trunk simultaneously. The third is the regenerative payload with an on-board bundle node, which converts the relay from a transponder into a router and is the hardware prerequisite for every protocol result in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. On the surface, the equivalent trend is power: once a node can operate through the night, on fission or hybrid sources, the topology no longer varies with illumination and the contact plan depends on orbital geometry alone.

Standards form the second axis of the roadmap. A 3GPP lunar NTN profile would require three features absent from all current releases: timers and hybrid automatic repeat request configurations beyond the GEO envelope for the trunk, timing advance without a navigation satellite fix, and store-and-forward extended from Internet-of-Things to broadband traffic. The LunaNet interoperability specification, CCSDS and the IETF delay-tolerant networking working group would need to converge on one security architecture with delay-tolerant key management. The ITU-R and the SFCG would need to resolve the Shielded Zone protection thresholds and the WRC-27 allocations before far-side constellations are designed, and to open the lunar plan above \SI{28.35}{GHz} (Section~\ref{sec:spectrum}). The corresponding research priorities are links and measurements in the first horizon, protocols and operations under intermittency in the second, and coverage extensions, autonomy and scale in the third.

The methodology extends beyond the Moon. The aperture-range scaling rests only on free-space propagation and applies to any cislunar or interplanetary link once the slant range is updated. At Mars the round-trip light time grows from \SI{2.56}{\second} to between \SI{6}{} and \SI{45}{minutes}, contact gaps extend from hours to days and, near solar conjunction, to weeks, and the delay-tolerant architecture surveyed here applies with longer timers and contact plans. The lunar network of the third horizon can therefore be a testbed for a Mars network, and the two can share relay payloads, protocols and ground segment.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Four of the six lunar links cross no atmosphere, and the band plan that will govern them is still being written (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). For these four links, the analysis yields one design rule. The orbit and the terrain are fixed first, because they set the range and the horizon. Each link then gets the carrier that its two ends can host, point and power, and the aperture-range law compares the candidate bands. Along the frequency axis, the rule leads to three findings. The Ka-band of the SFCG plan was adopted from the trunk to Earth without optimization for the lunar links. The links between pointable platforms gain about a factor of three from Ka-band to the range from E-band to about \SI{240}{GHz}, once the laboratory tubes of 2026 qualify. On the Moon, where no atmosphere constrains either choice, source power sets the upper limit of the radio ladder; pointing sets the choice between that limit and optics, with dust as a further constraint on optical terminals.

At the network level, the paper establishes three results. (i)~Two eccentric relays suffice for communications at the pole, whereas a navigation fix with a PDOP of at most 6 at 99.9~percent needs thirteen ELFO satellites in three planes over 21 days and eighteen over the full 90-day arc. Navigation therefore determines the size of the constellation. (ii)~Across the contact gaps, a traffic class is delivered in full only if its bundle lifetime exceeds the largest gap and its rate is within the capacity of the duty-cycled backhaul, and the scheduling policy matters only under congestion. (iii)~The boundary between the 3GPP surface and the delay-tolerant relay is a gateway function that no specification yet defines. Certificate validation on either side must also rely on revocation data held locally, because it cannot be retrieved from Earth in time.

For the satellite engineer the lunar relay link uses established radio techniques under intermittent connectivity; for the cellular engineer the lunar cell runs an established protocol stack, but terrain occlusion makes coverage binary and the relay duty cycle limits the backhaul capacity. The infrastructure now under contract was specified for a science-driven phase of about ten users on scheduled links; the sustained presence with several operators envisaged in current agency plans would require a two-tier constellation, repeaters on the illuminated ridges, a Moon-centric core and a shared far-side relay. The three horizons of the roadmap give the order in which these requirements are expected to be addressed. Because no in-situ propagation measurements of the lunar surface exist yet, every quantitative result in this paper that depends on terrain propagation requires validation by a surface measurement campaign.

\section{Design-Control Table and Code}
\label{app:dct}
Table~\ref{tab:dct} gives the design-control table for the six canonical links at the bands of Table~\ref{tab:links}, and for links~3 and~4 at E-band (\SI{81}{GHz}) and D-band (\SI{140}{GHz}). Every entry is computed from a single parameter set by the code distributed with the source of this paper, which also reproduces the trunk budgets of Section~\ref{sec:budgets}, the chain budget of Table~\ref{tab:chain} and the computed figures. Antenna gain follows \eqref{eq:gain} with $\eta = 0.6$, except at the DSN end, where the gain, beamwidth and system temperature of the handbook~\cite{dsn_34m_bwg} are used; the trunk includes the \SI{0.18}{dB} zenith atmosphere of Canberra in clear weather. Beamwidth follows $\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}} = 70\lambda/D$, and the pointing requirement is the error that costs \SI{0.5}{dB} at one end. The relay-side transmitters radiate \SI{10}{W}, so the E- and D-band columns isolate the effect of frequency at constant RF power; at these bands that power is delivered only by the laboratory tubes of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. The required $E_b/N_0$ is that of uncoded QPSK at a bit error rate of $10^{-5}$, and $L_{\mathrm{o}}$ lumps the implementation, polarization and pointing losses. At constant RF power, moving link~3 from Ka- to D-band raises its rate at a \SI{3}{dB} margin from 16 to \SI{153}{Mbps} and tightens the pointing requirement from \SI{0.33}{\degree} to \SI{0.061}{\degree}; link~4 rises from 21 to \SI{205}{Mbps}, with the pointing requirement tightening from \SI{0.16}{\degree} to \SI{0.031}{\degree}.
